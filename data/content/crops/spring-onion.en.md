## Starting
Spring onions thrive easily on a Dutch balcony. Choose straight bunching types like Ishikura or classic White Lisbon. These cold-hardy plants do not need to wait for IJsheiligen in mid-May. You can sow directly outside from March through August, as they tolerate frost down to –5 °C. Germination takes one to two weeks at 12–18 °C. For early harvests, start indoors in February by sowing clumps of four to eight seeds in module trays. Always use fresh seed, because onion seeds lose viability after one or two years. You can even regrow supermarket scrap roots in water or potting soil.

## Seedling
Seedlings emerge like fine blades of green grass. In square foot balcony planters, aim for sixteen individual plants or multi-sown clumps per square. If you direct sow in rows, space the furrows 15 to 20 cm apart. Thin young shoots gently so they stand 2.5 to 5 cm apart, or up to 10 cm for plumper stems. Enjoy all your thinned seedlings instantly as fresh microgreens. When transplanting module plugs, wait until shoots reach 5 to 10 cm and handle roots tenderly.

## Vegetative
Choose balcony window boxes or pots at least 15 to 20 cm deep with good drainage holes. Spring onions have shallow roots and thrive in rich, well-draining potting soil with a pH of 6.0 to 7.0. Place your containers in full sun or light half-shadow. Keep the soil evenly, moderately moist. Letting the soil dry out leads to tough stems and early bolting, while waterlogging rots roots. Avoid heavy nitrogen fertilizers to prevent limp foliage and pests. Instead, apply a little compost or potassium-rich vinassekali. Earth up around the base with loose compost to lengthen the sweet, blanched white stems without staking.

## Flowering
Drought or summer heat can cause plants to bolt into round, pale flower heads. These allium blossoms look lovely on a balcony and attract helpful bees and hoverflies. While the central stem turns tough and fibrous, the flower buds and petals are edible. Snip them into salads for a mild onion flavor. If you want seeds, leave blooms to dry completely on the stem until black seeds appear inside dry pods. Store them in an envelope in a cool, dark room.

## Harvest
Harvest your spring onions 8 to 12 weeks after sowing, once stems reach pencil thickness. You can gently loosen the potting soil and lift the whole plant, roots and all. Alternatively, try the cut-and-come-again method. Cut the greens about two centimeters above the soil line, leaving the base in place. The plant will push out fresh green growth for multiple rounds. Store harvested stems in your refrigerator crisper drawer wrapped in a damp cloth or breathable paper bag for 7 to 10 days. You can also stand roots upright in shallow fresh water on a cool indoor windowsill.

## FAQ
### How do I protect my balcony onions from onion fly and leek moth?
Maggots from onion flies and leek moths burrow into the stem base, causing wilting and yellow leaves. Shield your balcony pots with fine insect netting having a mesh size under 1.35 mm. If leek miner flies are present, use mesh of 0.8 mm or finer.

### Can I grow spring onions alongside carrots?
Yes, intercropping spring onions with carrots is an ideal companion planting combination. The scent of carrots confuses and disguises your onions from pests, while the aromatic onion foliage naturally deters carrot rust flies. Growing them together in the same container creates natural mutual defense.

### Why are my spring onion stems flopping over?
Stems often flop over when exposed to strong winds or when sown too densely without proper thinning. Weak stems struggle to stand upright. Earth up loose potting compost around the lower base for extra support, and ensure you thin crowded seedlings so each plant develops structural strength.

### How do I prevent rust and downy mildew on a Dutch balcony?
Damp, overcast Dutch weather can trigger orange rust pustules or greyish mould on foliage. To prevent fungal problems, place containers in a breezy, open balcony spot. Avoid overhead watering onto the leaves, and provide enough space between plants so air circulates freely around the stems.

### Can I plant spring onions in the same pot each year?
Avoid growing alliums in the same container soil in consecutive seasons. Repeating onions, garlic, leeks, or shallots in identical soil encourages nematodes and persistent root rot. Practice crop rotation by swapping your container soil or rotating plant families each season to maintain clean, pest-free growing conditions.

### How do I regrow spring onions from kitchen scraps?
Take supermarket spring onions and slice off the bottom root bases, keeping a few centimeters of stem intact. Stand them roots down in a shallow glass of water on a sunny indoor windowsill, or plant directly in moist potting compost. Fresh green stems will rapidly regenerate for cooking.

### What should I do if my plants bolt and form flower heads?
Bolting happens during warm dry spells or after winter cold. The central flowering stalk becomes fibrous and tough to chew, so harvest early. Do not throw the round blossoms away. The unopened buds and pale florets are edible and add a delightful, mild onion flavor to summer salads.

### What is the difference between Ishikura and White Lisbon?
In the Low Countries, bosui covers two distinct types. Ishikura is an Allium fistulosum bunching onion that stays slender and non-bulbing. White Lisbon belongs to Allium cepa, forming a mild, young-harvested bulb onion. Both grow splendidly in compact balcony containers and share similar care routines.

## Benefits
Spring onions bring fresh, crisp crunch and a mild, aromatic bite to balcony cooking. Both the tender white bases and hollow green tubular leaves are delicious. Slice them raw into rounds to brighten potato salads, cold carpaccio, creamy dips, and salad dressings. When cooking warm dishes, stir them into noodle broths, soups, and hot wok stir-fries during the final one or two minutes. Adding them right at the end preserves their vibrant green color and delicate, satisfying texture.
