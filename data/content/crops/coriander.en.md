## Starting
Coriander loves the crisp spring air of a Dutch balcony. Start sowing outdoors or in a cold frame as early as March. Unlike summer herbs, you do not need to wait for IJsheiligen. Gently crush the seed pods before sowing to crack the outer husk. Keep the soil moist and aerated. Seeds sprout in seven to ten days indoors, or two to three weeks in cool outdoor air. Sow small batches every three to four weeks for fresh leaves. Sow again in September for crisp autumn harvests.

## Seedling
Coriander develops a sensitive taproot that hates disturbance. Root shock triggers sudden flowering, so direct sowing into final pots is best. If starting indoors in March to dodge hungry slugs, sow two to four seeds per plug in light mix. Move the undisturbed root ball outside once plants show five true leaves. Planting clumps together creates a lush, bushy container. Avoid crowded supermarket pots, as their bound roots bolt almost immediately.

## Vegetative
Choose containers at least twenty to twenty-five centimeters deep with good drainage holes. Add hydro-pellets at the base and fill with compost-enriched potting mix. Dutch summer afternoons can scorch delicate leaves, so place containers in partial shade. Keep the soil evenly and consistently moist. Dry potting soil stresses roots and triggers rapid bolting, while soggy conditions harm root health. Feed lightly with liquid organic herb fertilizer every few weeks after the initial six weeks. As stems grow tall, insert a small bamboo cane to keep wind and rain from toppling the plant into wet soil.

## Flowering
Long summer days and warm weather naturally trigger flowering in June and July. Foliage turns fine and feathery like dill as stems rise up to sixty centimeters. Pretty umbrella clusters of white and pink blossoms soon open wide. Welcome these flowers on your balcony. They provide sweet nectar for wild bees, hoverflies, lacewings, and parasitic wasps that hunt aphids. Once blooms fade, small green capsules swell and ripen into aromatic, beige-brown seeds.

## Harvest
Clip outer leaves or stems when the plant reaches ten to fifteen centimeters tall. Harvesting from the outside inward encourages fresh side shoots. Pinch out emerging flower buds quickly to prolong leaf growth. Fresh leaves lose aroma if dried. Wrap fresh sprigs in a damp paper towel inside a sealed container in the fridge for up to a week, or freeze chopped leaves in oil. In late summer, cut stalks as seeds turn dry and light brown. Hang stems upside down inside a paper bag in a warm, dry room to collect falling seeds. Store fully dry seeds in airtight glass spice jars.

## FAQ
### Can I sow coriander before IJsheiligen in the Netherlands?
Yes, coriander tolerates light spring chills very well. You do not need to wait until May. Sow seeds outdoors or in a cold frame as early as March or April. Cool spring weather actually prevents premature bolting, giving you leafy, tender stems before summer heat arrives.

### Why does my coriander bolt and flower so quickly?
Coriander naturally flowers in reaction to stress, long summer days, dry potting soil, and high temperatures. Transplant shock or cramped root systems will also provoke fast bolting. Grow your plants in partial shade, keep moisture consistent, and avoid moving delicate root balls to delay blooming.

### Can I plant supermarket coriander pots into my garden?
It is best to avoid supermarket herb pots for your outdoor garden. These plants are forced rapidly in greenhouses and have crowded, tangled roots. The severe stress of transplanting makes them bolt to seed almost instantly. Sowing fresh seeds directly into your pots yields far better foliage.

### How do I protect young coriander seedlings from garden slugs?
Slugs love tender young coriander seedlings in Dutch gardens. Protect your crop by growing containers on raised balcony tables or plant stands elevated away from garden soil. You can also start seeds safely indoors in early spring or apply organic, iron-phosphate-based slug deterrents around your outdoor pots.

### Why do coriander leaves taste like soap to me?
A distinct genetic trait causes some people to perceive fresh coriander leaves as unpleasantly soapy. If you experience this flavor, do not toss the plant. Let it bloom and harvest the dry seeds instead. Coriander seeds lack those soapy compounds and offer a warm, citrus-spice flavor.

### What size container should I choose for growing coriander?
Choose a container with a minimum depth of twenty to twenty-five centimeters. Coriander produces a sensitive taproot that requires sufficient downward space to prevent stress. Ensure the pot has bottom drainage holes and add a base layer of hydro-pellets beneath compost-rich soil to prevent standing water.

### How often should I sow coriander for a continuous harvest?
Practice succession sowing every three to four weeks from spring through summer. Because individual plants run to flower quickly once mature, continuous sowing ensures you always have young, leafy stems ready for picking. Sow another batch in September for fresh autumn and winter pickings.

### How should I harvest and store the seeds?
Harvest the seeds in late summer when they turn light brown and dry on the stems. Cut the flower stalks and hang them upside down inside a paper bag in a warm, ventilated room. Once fully dried, store the whole seeds in airtight glass spice jars.

## Benefits
Coriander enlivens Mexican, Middle Eastern, and Asian dishes. Never toss the crunchy stems, as they hold intense aroma when pounded into curry pastes or marinades. Sprinkle fresh leaves raw over curries, soups, and garden omelets right before serving, as prolonged heat destroys their fragrance. Blend them with cashews and lime for vibrant pestos, or mix into a creamy Gooise dip. Lightly toast the dry seeds in a dry pan before crushing them into rubs, fragrant dals, and rich chermoula sauces.
