## Starting
Start your summer savory indoors from late March into April. Place pots on a warm, sunny windowsill or inside a cold greenhouse. These seeds need light to germinate properly. Scatter them thinly across moist seed-starting mix. Press them gently onto the surface. Do not bury them, though a faint dusting of fine sand works well. If you prefer sowing directly outside on your balcony, wait for late spring. Keep seeds protected from cold snaps. It is best to wait until mid-May after IJsheiligen has passed. This protects tender growth from late frosts and ensures steady, gentle warmth.

## Seedling
Watch for the first true leaves to appear on your young seedlings. Thin them out or prick them gently into individual containers. Ensure ample space between plants so fresh air circulates freely around the foliage. If you started seedlings indoors, harden them off gradually to the outdoor air. Move them to your balcony permanently only after IJsheiligen in mid-May. Keep young plants lifted off the ground during wet spring days to shield them from hungry slugs.

## Vegetative
Choose containers with drainage holes holding about three to five liters of soil. Summer savory thrives in a light, lean, and permeable soil mix. Blend in coarse sand or gravel to prevent waterlogged conditions. Avoid rich compost or heavy nitrogen fertilizers, as overfeeding creates weak growth and dilutes aromatic oils. Water in moderation. Allow the top soil layer to dry out between waterings. The plants stay naturally compact and bushy, reaching modest heights without trellising. Grow them in small clusters in your balcony planters so the slender stems can naturally support one another.

## Flowering
Summer savory blooms from July through September, displaying delicate pale pink, lilac, or white blossoms. These lovely flowers transform your balcony into a haven for pollinators. You will see wild bees, honeybees, hoverflies, and butterflies visiting regularly. For kitchen use, harvest your shoots right before or just as the first flowers open. At this moment, aromatic oils peak. Once plants set seed, the stems turn woodier and the fragrant leaves lose some of their signature punch.

## Harvest
Snip tender top shoots and leafy sprigs on dry mornings once morning dew clears. Regular cutting encourages your container plants to branch into bushier shapes. Summer savory naturally holds little moisture, making it exceptionally easy to air-dry. Hang small bundles upside down in a warm, dark, ventilated spot for two to three weeks. Alternatively, dry sprigs between paper towels in the microwave at six hundred watts for three minutes. Strip dry leaves into airtight glass jars away from light. You can also freeze sprigs in bags or steep them in olive oil and white wine vinegar.

## FAQ
### Why are my savory seeds failing to germinate?
Summer savory seeds need light to break dormancy. If you buried them under thick potting compost, they will not sprout. Scatter the seeds on top of damp seed-starting mix, press down lightly, and use only a sheer dusting of sand if needed. Keep the container warm and moist on a bright windowsill.

### When can I move my potted summer savory onto the balcony?
Keep seedlings indoors or in a greenhouse until after IJsheiligen in mid-May. Young plants are very sensitive to cold spring weather and frost. Once this date passes, harden the plants off gradually by exposing them to the outdoors for a few hours daily before leaving them outside permanently.

### What kind of potting mix works best in containers?
Summer savory hates wet feet and heavy compost. Use a light, free-draining potting blend mixed with coarse sand or fine gravel. Always pick containers with ample drainage holes at the bottom. This lean, permeable setup mimics natural conditions, preventing soggy roots while keeping the foliage robust and deeply flavorful.

### How should I water summer savory on a Dutch balcony?
Practice moderation with your watering can. Balcony containers dry out during hot spells, but overwatering causes root rot. Feel the soil first and allow the top few centimeters to dry out before watering again. Never let the pot sit in stagnant water collected in an undrained balcony saucer.

### Do I need to fertilize my plants regularly?
No, you should avoid heavy feeding and high-nitrogen fertilizers. Summer savory actually prefers leaner growing conditions. Overfeeding causes rapid, floppy growth and dilutes the concentration of essential aromatic oils. A lean potting mix produces sturdier stems and ensures your culinary harvest packs maximum savory flavor in every leaf.

### Can summer savory help other plants on my balcony?
Yes, it makes a wonderful companion plant alongside broad beans and French beans. Summer savory gives off a pungent, spicy scent that naturally confuses and repels black bean aphids. Planting a small pot of savory right beside your potted beans keeps pests away naturally without any chemical treatments.

### How do I protect young seedlings from garden pests?
Slugs love tender young savory seedlings during damp Dutch spring weather. To keep them safe, elevate your seed trays or small pots off the ground on balcony railings, tables, or plant stands. Once the seedlings mature and develop tougher, aromatic leaves, slugs generally leave the plants alone.

### How do I preserve summer savory for winter cooking?
Bundle harvested stems and hang them upside down in a warm, dark, airy room for two to three weeks. You can also dry sprigs between paper towels in the microwave. Once crisp, strip the leaves and store them in an airtight jar away from light for up to one year.

## Benefits
Summer savory brings a warm, peppery punch to small-space culinary gardens. In Dutch kitchens, it is the classic pairing for green beans, runner beans, broad beans, and dried pulses. It is an essential seasoning in the traditional dish "Blote billetjes in het gras." You can stir chopped leaves into rich vegetable stews, hearty lentil soups, or tempeh marinades. Blend it into herb butter, craft homemade Herbes de Provence mixes, or steep the sprigs for fragrant herbal tea.
