## Starting
Broccoli hates fluctuating soil moisture, so begin indoors or in a cold greenhouse. Start your seeds between late March and late June, or sow until mid-July for an autumn crop. Use modular trays or nine-centimetre pots filled with rich compost rather than tiny cells. Place up to three seeds per pot. Keep the containers in a bright, cool spot around fifteen degrees Celsius. With steady conditions, your seeds will sprout in seven to ten days. Starting in larger starter pots shields the tender roots and prevents the soil from drying out fast.

## Seedling
When seedlings reach three centimetres tall, thin them out by snipping away weaker shoots. Leave only one sturdy seedling per pot. Acclimatise your young plants outdoors in a sheltered, semi-shaded spot so they adjust to outdoor air. Avoid sudden temperature shocks, which cause young brassicas to bolt. Once seedlings stand six to eight centimetres tall with several true leaves, they are ready for their permanent containers. Planting out is safest around mid-May after IJsheiligen passes.

## Vegetative
Choose compact varieties like Montebello F1 or Solid Dwarf for pots and small spaces. Give each plant its own container measuring at least twenty-five centimetres across with good drainage. Broccoli feeds heavily. Fill your pots with organic potting compost and provide extra organic nitrogen and potassium feed during growth. Balcony pots dry out rapidly in Dutch summer heat, leading to limp heads, so keep the compost consistently moist. Anchor mature plants against gusty balcony winds by inserting a firm bamboo cane beside the main stem. Happy vegetative growth builds the energy needed for full crown development.

## Flowering
Your broccoli crown is actually a dense cluster of tiny, unopened flower buds. Head formation thrives in mild temperatures between sixteen and twenty-two degrees Celsius. Keep watering steady during this phase. If summer heat rises past twenty-five degrees Celsius or the potting soil dries out, the head will loosen. The buds will rapidly separate and burst into open yellow flowers. Shield the pot from scorching sun during heatwaves to preserve tight, solid crowns.

## Harvest
Harvest the central head while buds remain dark green, dense, and firm, typically ten to fifteen centimetres across. Cut the main stalk cleanly with about ten centimetres of tender stem attached. Never pull out the plant after this first harvest. Within one to two weeks, productive side shoots will emerge from the leaf joints. You can harvest these sweet smaller florets for several weeks. Fresh broccoli keeps loosely wrapped in the refrigerator drawer for three to five days. For long-term storage, blanch florets in boiling water for two to three minutes before freezing, or preserve them in vinegar pickles.

## FAQ
### How can I protect my balcony broccoli from caterpillars?
The large and small cabbage white butterflies are the biggest threat. Protect your crop by covering plants from day one with fine insect netting or garden fleece over supporting hoops. Physical exclusion stops the butterflies from landing on the foliage and laying eggs. Check underneath leaves occasionally just in case any stray pests slipped beneath the netting.

### What causes broccoli heads to become loose and rubbery?
Rubbery heads, known as slappe broccoli, stem directly from dry container soil or excessive summer heat. Balcony pots dry out rapidly on sunny days. When moisture dips or temperatures exceed twenty-five degrees Celsius, the bud clusters separate instead of packing tightly. Maintain evenly damp potting mix and move containers into lighter shade during heatwaves.

### How do I stop cabbage root fly from attacking my plants?
The cabbage root fly lays eggs right at the base of the plant stem. Emerging larvae burrow downward and destroy the root system. Place a flat rubber or cardboard cabbage collar directly onto the compost around the stem base of each seedling. This barrier effectively prevents adult flies from depositing eggs into the nearby soil.

### What pot size is best for growing broccoli in small spaces?
Select a container with a diameter of at least twenty-five centimetres, providing roughly ten to fifteen litres of volume per plant. Ensure the container has drainage holes to avoid waterlogged soil. Compact varieties such as Montebello F1 or Solid Dwarf suit these pot dimensions well and stay manageable on balconies.

### Can I still eat the broccoli if yellow flowers appear?
Yes, the florets remain completely edible once tiny yellow blossoms emerge. Bolting happens due to intense warmth or dry roots. While safe to eat, flowering stems become somewhat more fibrous and carry a slightly bitter taste. Cut the head immediately to prevent further flowering and encourage the plant to send out fresh side shoots.

### How does container gardening prevent clubroot?
Clubroot is a damaging soil-borne organism that distorts roots and stunts growth in ground beds. It requires long crop rotations to control. Container growing bypasses this problem because you fill pots with fresh, clean commercial potting compost. As long as you use fresh compost, the pathogen cannot infect your balcony vegetables.

### When is it safe to move seedlings outdoors permanently?
Young plants tolerate cool spring air once hardened off, but cold shock can induce premature flowering. It is safest to move young plants into permanent outdoor pots around mid-May, right after the IJsheiligen frost period passes. Ensure your seedlings are six to eight centimetres tall and acclimatised before placing them outside full-time.

### Why should I leave the broccoli plant in the pot after harvesting the head?
Cutting the central head does not finish the plant's life cycle. If you leave the stalk in its pot, new baby florets will sprout from the leaf axils within one to two weeks. These tender secondary shoots yield continuous, tasty pickings for several weeks, significantly boosting your harvest from a single container.

## Benefits
Broccoli offers versatile cooking options right from your balcony harvest. Enjoy the tender side florets steamed or boiled for three to five minutes until al dente. Do not discard the thick main stalk; simply peel away the tough outer skin and slice the sweet inner core into stir-fries. You can even harvest the young leaves to cook like kale or collards. For quick meals, toss slender side shoots in olive or sesame oil and pan-sear over high heat.
