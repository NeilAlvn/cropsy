## Starting
Summer purslane loves warmth. The seeds need warm soil to sprout, ideally eighteen to twenty degrees. You can pre-sow indoors in trays from March or April at twenty degrees. For outdoor balcony containers or small garden beds, wait until after IJsheiligen in mid-May. Night frost will harm this crop. Continue successive sowings through June, July, and into early August for a continuous supply. The seeds are very small. Scatter them across the surface and press them in lightly, or top with a tiny dusting of fine soil or vermiculite. Keep the soil surface evenly moist until your seeds germinate.

## Seedling
Young seedlings have delicate, shallow roots that dislike disturbance. It is best to sow them directly in their final balcony containers or raised beds. If you want quick cut-and-come-again baby leaves, you can keep the growth dense without much thinning. For larger, sprawling leafy plants, thin your seedlings to ten to fifteen centimeters apart. Take care when handling the tender sprouts. Ensure good air circulation and bright light so they develop sturdy stems.

## Vegetative
Summer purslane thrives in full sun. Because of shallow roots, containers only need a depth of fifteen to twenty centimeters, such as a five to ten liter pot. Use light, well-drained potting soil mixed with sand and humus. The fleshy, semi-succulent leaves handle summer heat and brief dry spells well. In balcony containers, water directly at the base so the soil does not bake dry. This keeps the stems thick and tender. Feed lightly with compost or general organic fertilizer. Avoid heavy nitrogen feeding. The plants form branching mounds reaching twenty to forty centimeters high without needing any stakes or supports.

## Flowering
Small star-shaped yellow flowers emerge in the leaf axils during hot summer weather from July to August. Heat and drought naturally encourage flowering. Once the plant flowers, stems turn fibrous and the leaves develop a stronger tart flavor. These plants are self-pollinating annuals. They form small capsules containing tiny black seeds. Seeds mature rapidly, and the plants will gladly self-sow if you let the capsules open. Keep plants watered if you want to delay flowering.

## Harvest
In warm weather, summer purslane grows very quickly. Your crop is ready for harvest three to four weeks after sowing. Use clean scissors to cut the stems three to four centimeters above the soil. Always leave the lowest leaves intact. The plant will soon produce fresh side shoots for a second or third cut before flowering starts. Purslane wilts quickly after picking. It is best to cut stems directly before eating. If needed, wrap the leaves in a damp cloth or a perforated bag. Keep them in your refrigerator drawer at four to six degrees for up to two days.

## FAQ
### Why did my summer purslane seeds fail to germinate?
Summer purslane seeds need warm soil to sprout properly. Cold or wet spring weather stops germination completely. If you sow outdoors before IJsheiligen in mid-May, the soil temperature is usually too low. Wait for sunny weather or start seeds indoors at twenty degrees. Keep the soil surface gently moist.

### What causes stem or root rot in container plants?
Stagnant moisture causes root decay and damping-off because the fleshy stems cannot handle soggy soil. This issue happens quickly in containers without enough drainage holes. Always choose pots that let excess water escape freely. Use a light, well-draining potting mix, and pour water directly onto the soil rather than wetting the leaves.

### How can I protect young seedlings from slugs?
Tender young purslane leaves attract garden slugs, especially during wet spells. Growing your plants on a balcony or in elevated containers creates a natural barrier against these pests. Check your pots regularly in the evening or after rain. Keeping containers lifted off garden soil provides good protection for your seedlings.

### Why is my purslane flowering so early?
Intense heat spikes and dry soil push purslane into premature flowering. Once the plant feels severe drought, it rushes to produce seeds. To prevent early bolting, keep the soil moderately watered during hot spells. Adding a light layer of mulch across the pot surface helps retain steady soil moisture on warm balconies.

### Can I grow summer purslane in the shade?
Summer purslane is a heat-loving vegetable that performs best in full sun. It relies on bright light and warmth to build thick, succulent stems and lush foliage. Balconies or garden spots in heavy shade remain too cool and dim, causing weak, leggy growth. Place your pots in the sunniest outdoor spot available.

### What size pot is best for a balcony?
Summer purslane has shallow roots, so you do not need deep planters. A pot depth of fifteen to twenty centimeters is plenty. A five to ten liter balcony container or a standard thirty-centimeter square plot gives the root system ample space to support healthy, branching growth throughout the summer season.

### How much fertilizer should I give the plants?
Summer purslane is a low to moderate feeder. A light amount of compost or organic vegetable fertilizer mixed into the soil before planting is completely sufficient. Avoid giving heavy nitrogen fertilizers. Purslane easily absorbs and stores excess nitrates from rich plant foods, so keep feeding gentle and balanced.

### How do I store freshly harvested purslane?
The tender leaves wilt quickly once cut from the plant. Harvest stems right before cooking or preparing meals. If you must store them, tuck the stems inside a damp cloth or a perforated bag. Place them in your refrigerator drawer at four to six degrees, where they keep for one to two days.

### How do I harvest purslane for cut-and-come-again crops?
Cut the stems three to four centimeters above the potting soil using sharp scissors. Always make sure to leave the lowest leaves behind on the plant base. New side shoots will quickly branch out from these leaves, allowing you to harvest fresh greens a second or even third time.

## Benefits
Summer purslane brings a crisp, juicy texture and a refreshing, slightly sour and salty taste to your kitchen. Enjoy raw sprigs in crisp summer salads with feta, walnuts, courgette, and olive oil vinaigrette. You can also cook the stems gently like spinach or toss them into hot stir-fries. In the Netherlands, cooks stir fresh leaves into warm potato stamppot at the very end. The plant also enriches light vegetable broths, where its natural juices add smooth body without cream.
