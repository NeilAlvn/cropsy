## Starting
Starting shallots on your balcony is wonderfully straightforward with plant sets. These robust sets split into generous clusters of bulbs. You can plant them outside between late February and mid-April. They handle frost well, so you never have to wait for IJsheiligen. Red types enjoy an early start in February. Plant yellow varieties from late March to avoid cold, wet conditions that trigger bolting. You can also plant hardy types in autumn. Press each bulb base down with the pointed tip peeking slightly out of the soil. In square-foot garden boxes, place four to nine sets per grid square.

## Seedling
If you grow shallots from seed, begin indoors or in a cold greenhouse between late January and early March. Maintain temperatures around 15 to 18 °C on a sunny windowsill. Thin grassy shoots to one sturdy plant per module once they emerge after two to three weeks. Harden the young plugs off gradually. Transplant them into your outdoor pots around late March or April once the root system has formed a firm, supportive root plug.

## Vegetative
Give your shallots containers at least twenty to twenty-five centimeters deep with ample drainage holes. Water moderately, keeping to the rule that drier is better than too wet. Keep soil lightly moist during spring green growth, then ease off watering in July. Shallots are frugal feeders. Never use rich compost, fresh manure, or excessive nitrogen, which prompts excessive leaves, mildew, and rotting. Instead, add potassium like vinassekorrels or a dusting of wood ash around neutral soil conditions to build tough skins. The upright hollow stems reach thirty to forty centimeters tall and need no extra support.

## Flowering
Shallots may bolt if they endure a cold snap followed by sudden heat or drought. The thick flower stalk diverts vital energy away from the bulb, leaving behind a hard, woody core. Snap or cut off any emerging flower stalks immediately. Harvest and eat these bolted shallots right away, as they will not store well. If left in place, the spherical flower heads attract visiting bees and pollinators, but you will sacrifice your kitchen harvest.

## Harvest
Spring sets are ready between July and early August. Harvest begins when two-thirds of the foliage turns yellow, wilts, and collapses naturally onto the soil. Gently lift the bulb clusters on a dry, sunny day. Allow them to dry in the sun for several days, or hang them in a well-ventilated shed or porch for two to three weeks. Wait until necks are dry and skins crackle like paper. Separate the clusters or braid the dried leaves together. Store them in mesh bags or crates in a dark, dry, frost-free room between 5 and 12 °C for up to eight to twelve months.

## FAQ
### Why do birds pull out my planted sets?
Blackbirds, magpies, and pigeons frequently spot exposed bulb tips peeking through the compost, mistaking them for juicy earthworms. Protect newly potted containers by covering them with chicken wire, fine netting, or protective mesh baskets until roots anchor the shallots firmly in place.

### Can I grow shallots in window boxes or small patio pots?
Yes, shallots have shallow roots and thrive in balcony containers. Choose pots at least twenty to twenty-five centimeters deep and twenty-five to thirty centimeters wide with generous drainage holes. Ensure water drains freely away so the multiplying bulb clusters never sit in stagnant, soggy soil.

### How do I protect shallots from the onion fly?
The onion fly and leek mining fly lay damaging eggs near the plant base, allowing larvae to burrow into bulbs. Cover your planters with fine insect netting smaller than 0.8 millimeters, or companion plant with carrots so their mingling scents confuse both pests effectively.

### What causes shallot bulbs to rot in containers?
Rot and fungal issues like downy mildew usually stem from poor container drainage, planting bulbs too deeply, or overfeeding with nitrogen. Use fast-draining potting soil, keep watering light, avoid fresh manure, and wait four to five years before reusing potting soil for allium crops.

### Why should I plant yellow shallots later than red ones?
Red varieties like Red Sun tolerate early planting from February. Yellow varieties like Strogele should wait until late March or April. Placing yellow sets into cold, wet spring soil can trigger premature bolting, causing plants to produce flower stems instead of large, dividing bulb clusters.

### Do shallots need lots of fertilizer or rich compost?
Shallots are frugal feeders that dislike heavy nutrients. Too much nitrogen or rich compost encourages leafy green growth at the expense of bulbs and spoils storage quality. Instead, supply potassium using vinassekorrels or a dusting of wood ash around a neutral soil pH level.

### How much water do container shallots need?
Follow the golden rule: better too dry than too wet. Keep soil lightly moist during active spring leaf production. As bulbs mature during July, pull back watering significantly. Dry conditions in late summer help prevent bulb rot and ensure firm outer skins for long-term pantry storage.

### What is the difference between plant sets and seeds?
Plant sets are the most reliable option for home gardeners, reliably splitting into a generous cluster of bulbs during a single season. Growing from seed requires a much longer growing season, earlier indoor sowing, and often produces single large bulbs or smaller splits instead of clusters.

## Benefits
Shallots bring a sweet, mild, and aromatic depth to the kitchen without the harsh bite of common onions. Mince them raw into classic vinaigrettes, fresh salads, and savory tartares. They serve as an essential base for French pan sauces like béarnaise, rich tomato chutneys, and cozy soups. You can also roast whole or halved bulbs in olive oil and balsamic vinegar until sticky and caramelized, or fry thin slices crisp to crown bean dishes and noodle bowls.
