## Starting
Wild rocket is a hardy perennial that thrives on your balcony. You do not need to wait for mid-May frost markers to begin. Direct outdoor sowing works best from late February or March until September. Starting seeds indoors in warm rooms creates pale, spindly seedlings, so take your trays straight outside. Scatter the tiny seeds shallowly beneath a thin dusting of sand or compost. Keep the soil surface consistently moist. Seeds take two to three weeks to germinate in chilly early spring weather. Once temperatures settle between 10 °C and 20 °C, green shoots pop up in six to ten days.

## Seedling
Seedlings often emerge in thick little bunches. Thin them boldly so your rocket has room to breathe. Leave just one sturdy plant per spot to avoid crowded leaves. If you started seeds in small pots, keep small clumps of four or five seedlings together. Transfer these clusters directly into your containers without disturbing the fragile roots. Give individual plants about 10 cm of breathing room. Good airflow keeps the foliage healthy and gives each low rosette plenty of space to spread out nicely.

## Vegetative
Wild rocket develops a deep taproot, so choose containers at least 20 to 25 cm deep with drainage holes. Window boxes also work nicely. Give your pots plenty of sunshine. Move them into partial shade during hot summer spells to prevent leaves from turning unpleasantly bitter. Water the soil around the base steadily to prevent premature bolting, keeping the leaves dry. Wild rocket loves well-drained, sandy soil topped with a centimetre of garden compost. Skip heavy synthetic nitrogen feeds, which only create weak, sappy growth that invites pests. The low rosettes stay compact without any stakes or supports.

## Flowering
From May through September, plants send up tall stems carrying vivid, golden-yellow four-petaled flowers. These cheerful blooms attract bumblebees, hoverflies, and solitary bees to your balcony. Slender seed pods quickly follow insect pollination. Wild rocket readily self-seeds in open pot soil and balcony cracks. Snap off the flowering stems before the pods dry out if you wish to prevent unwanted spread. Do not let them go to waste, though. Both the bright yellow flowers and young green pods are edible, offering a sweet, concentrated peppery punch.

## Harvest
Begin harvesting tender leaves three to five weeks after sowing, once they reach eight to ten centimetres long. Never pull the entire plant from the soil. Instead, pinch or snip outer leaves one or two centimetres above the soil line. Keep the central growing heart intact so new flushes can sprout all season long. Use your freshly clipped greens right away for the best texture. You can store leaves in the fridge for three to four days wrapped in a damp paper towel. In late autumn, growth slows down, but the hardy root crown survives freezing winter weather to sprout again come March.

## FAQ
### Why is my wild rocket blooming so early?
Extended daylight and warm summer temperatures trigger wild rocket to bloom early. Dry soil also stresses the plant into flowering. Keep your potting soil consistently damp and shift your containers into a cooler, partially shaded spot during hot summer spells to slow down flower production and preserve leaf flavor.

### What is eating small round holes in my leaves?
Tiny jumping flea beetles love chewing small round holes through dry brassica leaves. These pests thrive in dry, dusty dirt. Keep your soil surface consistently humid by watering regularly to deter them. You can also cover your plants with ultra-fine insect mesh to physically shield the foliage.

### How can I protect my rocket from caterpillars?
Large and small cabbage white butterflies lay eggs on cruciferous greens. Their caterpillars quickly devour foliage. Protect your containers with fine mesh netting to keep butterflies off. You can also inspect leaf undersides regularly, plucking away clusters of yellow eggs and green caterpillars by hand before they do harm.

### Will garden slugs eat my wild rocket plants?
Slugs generally steer clear of mature wild rocket foliage. The leaves contain high concentrations of pungent mustard glucosinolates that taste far too sharp for them compared to mild lettuces. However, very young, tender seedlings emerging in early spring still need basic protection until they develop their strong, spicy bite.

### Can wild rocket survive freezing winter conditions?
Yes, wild rocket is a true perennial that easily tolerates Dutch winters. Growth slows down or dies back in late autumn as temperatures drop. However, the root crown remains alive through frosty weather beneath the soil. It will wake up and send out very early fresh greens the following March.

### Should I start wild rocket seeds on a warm indoor windowsill?
Direct outdoor sowing is much better. When started in warm living rooms, wild rocket seedlings quickly turn pale, thin, and spindly from excessive warmth. The seeds are naturally cold-hardy, so you can sow them directly outside in pots as early as late February without waiting for warmer weather.

### How does wild rocket differ from annual cultivated rocket?
Wild rocket is a long-lived perennial featuring narrow, deeply incised foliage and bright, golden-yellow four-petaled flowers. Standard annual rocket produces broader leaves and cream-coloured flowers marked with violet veins. Wild rocket also packs a far sharper, punchier mustard flavour and regrows reliably year after year from its established root crown.

### Can I eat the yellow flowers and seed pods?
Yes, both the yellow flowers and the slender, immature seed pods are completely edible. They carry a sweet, concentrated peppery punch that brightens up plates. Toss them into fresh salads or use them as a colourful garnish. Harvesting the flowers also stops the plant from scattering seeds across your balcony.

## Benefits
Wild rocket brings a sharp, mustardy, and nutty kick to the kitchen. Scatter fresh leaves over carpaccio, slide them into sandwiches with aged cheese, or toss them onto hot pizza right before serving. Never boil or stew the leaves like spinach. Instead, stir them into hot risottos, pasta, or traditional Dutch mashed potato stamppot at the final second to gently wilt them. If older summer leaves turn overly pungent, blend them with garlic, toasted nuts, cheese, and olive oil for a vibrant homemade pesto.
