## Starting
You can grow garden cress indoors all year round on a bright windowsill. For simple sprouts, moist cotton wool, paper towels, or hydro pebbles work well. Sowing on potting soil gives sturdier stems and a fuller flavour. Outdoors, start in early spring from late February to mid-April, or wait until September. These seeds sprout easily in cool weather and do not require waiting for the Ice Saints. Avoid hot summer sowing between June and August, as heat causes rapid bolting. The seeds need light to germinate. Scatter them evenly across the damp surface and gently press them down without burying them. Expect germination within three to seven days.

## Seedling
Pricking out is rarely necessary for garden cress. Because you usually enjoy it as young shoots, sow seeds close together in your tray. If you want mature, serrated leaves outdoors, thin your young seedlings to leave two to five centimetres of space. You can also start seeds in tiny pots and transplant them gently in clusters into larger containers. Always handle tender roots with extra care to prevent setback. Keep the baby greens shaded from hot, intense afternoon light.

## Vegetative
Roots remain shallow throughout growth. Use shallow dishes for sprouts, or choose pots at least twelve centimetres deep for mature greens. A moisture-retentive potting compost works best. Position your pots in gentle partial shade outside or an east- or west-facing windowsill indoors. Keep the soil evenly moist at every moment, as dry roots will wither immediately. Water gently with a spray bottle or fine rose to avoid flattening tiny stems. Always use containers with drainage holes to avoid standing water and root rot. Extra plant food is never needed; the seedlings gather all their nutrition directly from the seed itself during this brief cycle.

## Flowering
Rising warmth above twenty degrees and lengthening days will push cress into flower. Stems shoot upwards to fifty centimetres, bearing delicate white blossoms. Do not throw these away, as the tiny blooms are edible with a pleasant, peppery snap. If you let seed pods mature and dry out in late summer, you can harvest and thresh them. Store these fresh seeds carefully in a cool spot for your next home-grown container crop.

## Harvest
Harvest microgreens after five to ten days once the first two seed leaves unfold fully. If you prefer adult foliage, wait three to four weeks until leaves develop their distinct serrated shape. Always snip the stems with clean kitchen scissors just above the soil or cotton wool. Do not pull plants out by the roots, keeping your harvest clean from compost debris. Enjoy your cress fresh for the best crunch and sharp flavour. Stored in a sealed box with moist paper towel, it keeps in the fridge for two days. Never dry or freeze the stems. Sow successive batches every week for a non-stop supply.

## FAQ
### Is white fuzz on my seedlings dangerous mold?
It is often not mold at all. Healthy young sprouting cress naturally develops tiny, fuzzy white root hairs along its emerging taproot. True mold forms a web-like gray or white fuzz across the leaves and compost. This happens if air circulation is poor or moisture is excessive. Ensure fresh airflow and avoid drowning your seeds.

### Why are my cress stems growing tall, pale, and weak?
This elongation happens when seedlings grow in an area that is too warm and too dim. The tiny plants stretch rapidly towards any light source, leaving stems weak and floppy. Shift your container immediately to a brighter spot, such as an east- or west-facing windowsill, and maintain cooler temperatures around fifteen to eighteen degrees.

### Why are the leaves turning yellow on my container plants?
Yellowing foliage typically points to two main issues: insufficient light or soggy soil. Waterlogged compost suffocates tender roots quickly. Always select containers with drainage holes so surplus water flows away freely. Move the pot to a bright position with good daylight, avoiding direct midsummer sun, and let the soil breathe between waterings.

### Do I need to practice crop rotation when growing cress?
If planting in garden soil, yes. Garden cress belongs to the brassica family, making it vulnerable to soil-borne problems like clubroot. Keep a four- to six-year rotation away from cabbage and radishes. However, on balconies and patios using pots filled with fresh potting compost, crop rotation is completely unnecessary.

### Should I add liquid fertilizer to my garden cress?
You never need to add fertilizer to garden cress. Young sprouts rely entirely on the stored nutrients within the seed coat. Even if you choose to grow mature leaves, standard potting mix provides more than enough food for the plant's brief growing cycle. Extra feeding is wasteful and unnecessary.

### Why should I avoid sowing cress during midsummer outdoors?
High temperatures and long summer days force garden cress into premature bolting. If you sow outdoors during June, July, or August, the heat easily scorches delicate emerging shoots or triggers instant flowering before useful leaves grow. Limit outdoor balcony sowings to early spring and the cooler days of September.

### Can I preserve extra cress by freezing or drying it?
Freezing and drying do not work for garden cress. The delicate cell structure collapses completely when frozen, while drying strips away its sharp, peppery punch and fresh crispness. Harvest only what you need right before eating. For a constant harvest, sow fresh trays every week rather than storing old cuts.

### Are garden cress flowers safe and tasty to eat?
Yes, the tiny white flowers are completely edible. They carry a mild, pleasant peppery flavour just like the leaves. You can scatter the blooms over open sandwiches, fresh salads, or creamy dips as a decorative touch. Letting a few stems flower also allows you to harvest seeds later on.

## Benefits
Garden cress delivers a sharp, mustard-like pepperiness reminiscent of fresh radish and watercress. Always enjoy it raw to preserve its crunchy texture and spicy punch. Heat quickly turns the leaves limp and unpleasantly bitter, so fold it into hot soups or mashed potatoes just before serving. It shines on buttered bread, toast with mature cheese, or alongside boiled eggs and hummus. Chop fresh cress straight into homemade mayonnaise, creamy herb butter, quark, or crisp lunch salads.
