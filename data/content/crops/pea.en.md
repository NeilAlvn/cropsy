## Starting
Peas thrive in crisp Dutch spring weather. You can start them indoors from late January through February in small pots. Direct balcony sowing runs smoothly from late February to mid-April. Soaking your seeds in water for twelve to twenty-four hours softens their wrinkled coats and speeds up sprouting. Never sow soaked seeds into waterlogged soil to prevent rot. Peas germinate between 7 °C and 20 °C, taking one to two weeks indoors. Starting in containers indoors or in a cold frame safely protects early seeds from hungry mice, birds, and chilly spring downpours. They are naturally frost-hardy and love this early seasonal start.

## Seedling
Peas have fragile taproots that dislike disturbance. Never prick them out bare-root. Sow them into module trays, small pots, or paper rolls so you can transplant the root ball completely intact. Once seedlings stand five to ten centimeters tall in March or early April, harden them off outdoors in a sheltered spot. Established pea plants tolerate light night frosts down to minus five degrees Celsius. If severe freezing snaps threaten, simply cover them with protective fleece until morning.

## Vegetative
Give your balcony peas a pot at least twenty to twenty-five centimeters deep and wide. Ensure containers have generous drainage holes. Keep the soil evenly moist because balcony pots dry out fast, but avoid waterlogging. Peas host root bacteria that fix nitrogen, so avoid nitrogen-rich fertilizers. Too much nitrogen spurs leafy green growth while reducing blooms. Standard potting compost provides plenty of food. Dwarf varieties reach fifty to seventy centimeters and need only short stakes, twigs, or small grids. Tall climbing varieties grow up to two meters. Provide these climbers with a sturdy trellis, bamboo teepee, or wire mesh. Gently train young stems toward supports so their tendrils can climb.

## Flowering
Pretty butterfly-shaped blossoms open in May. Peas are self-fertile, so wind alone sets fruit easily, though bumblebees often visit. Moderate temperatures between 15 °C and 20 °C are ideal for healthy fruit set. Hot summer spikes above 25 °C make flowers drop and halt development, making the temperate Dutch spring climate optimal. Increase your container watering during this stage to help pods fill out nicely. Consistent moisture keeps the plants productive and stress-free.

## Harvest
Harvest arrives around June and July, roughly nine to twelve weeks after sowing. Pick when pods look plump and round, with firm peas packed inside. If pods turn dull and yellow, the peas have turned tough and mealy. Always pick using two hands or sharp scissors. One hand holds the vine while the other detaches the pod, preventing fragile stems from snapping. Once picked, sugars convert into starch within hours. Eat them immediately for peak sweetness. Otherwise, store unshelled pods in a perforated fridge bag for two to three days. For long storage, shell, blanch for two to three minutes, cool in ice water, and freeze.

## FAQ
### Do I need to wait until IJsheiligen to move my peas outside?
Peas are hardy cool-season vegetables, so you do not need to wait for IJsheiligen in mid-May. Established plants comfortably endure night frosts between minus three and minus five degrees. They often go outside one to two months before mid-May. If rare severe freezes arrive, simply cover them with protective fleece temporarily.

### How do I protect balcony peas from birds and mice?
Pigeons and mice frequently dig up planted seeds and strip delicious young shoots. Shield your containers with wire mesh, bird netting, or garden fleece immediately after sowing. Keep this physical barrier in place until seedlings reach roughly thirty to thirty-five centimeters tall and become sturdier against pests.

### Why are the lower leaves on my pea plants turning yellow?
Yellow leaves on container peas usually point to overwatering or poor drainage in the potting soil. Make sure containers drain freely and never sit in pooled water. Later in the season, yellowing at the bottom of the vines is natural senescence as the plants finish maturing their pods.

### How do I prevent powdery mildew on my peas?
Powdery mildew shows up as a white powdery film on pea foliage during warm, dry weather late in the season. The best prevention is sowing early in February or March so plants finish before peak mildew weather. In addition, maintain proper airflow around your containers and avoid drought stress.

### What causes small caterpillars inside my pea pods?
Caterpillars inside pods are the larvae of the pea moth. These pests burrow into the green pods and feed directly on the developing peas. Sowing your peas early in spring ensures pods set and ripen well before adult pea moths emerge during June and July.

### What type of support should I give container peas?
Support depends on whether you grow dwarf or climbing peas. Dwarf varieties reach fifty to seventy centimeters and only need short twigs, stakes, or small cages. Tall climbing varieties grow up to two meters and need a sturdy trellis, wire mesh, or bamboo teepee to grip with their tendrils.

### How much fertilizer do peas need in containers?
Peas are light feeders and host Rhizobium bacteria on their roots to fix nitrogen from the air. Avoid high-nitrogen fertilizers completely, as excess nitrogen creates lush green leaves but fewer blossoms and pods. Normal potting compost provides all the baseline nutrients your plants need for the season.

### Can I eat the outer pods of garden peas?
Standard garden pea pods contain a tough, fibrous parchment layer on the inside, so you should shell them and eat only the round seeds. However, young tender shoot tips, tendrils, and flowers are completely edible. Snip the top shoots to toss into fresh salads and stir-fries.

## Benefits
Unlike mangetout, standard garden peas have a tough, fibrous inner pod membrane, so you only eat the shelled green peas. Freshly picked peas taste wonderful raw. If cooking, steam or boil them in lightly salted water for just two to four minutes to protect their natural crunch and bright color. You can also harvest the tender top five to ten centimeters of leafy shoots and curling tendrils. Add these sweet shoots and edible flowers to fresh salads and stir-fries.
