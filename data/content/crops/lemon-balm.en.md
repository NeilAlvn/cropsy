## Starting
Start your lemon balm indoors from late March or April. A warm windowsill at normal room temperature works nicely. The fine seeds need light to germinate. Press them gently onto moist seed compost without covering them with soil. If you prefer outdoor sowing, wait until mid-May when the risk of night frost passes after the Ice Saints. You can also start plants without seeds. Divide an established mature clump in spring or autumn using a spade. Alternatively, root semi-ripe shoots in a glass of plain water until healthy roots appear. Both methods establish very quickly in pots.

## Seedling
Watch for your seedlings to produce two to four true leaves. When they are easy to handle, gently prick them out into individual pots filled with nutritious organic herb compost. Early in May, begin hardening off the young plants. Gradually introduce them to balcony breezes and outdoor temperatures. Move them outside permanently once the Ice Saints conclude in mid-May. Give them plenty of room to expand, as each small plant quickly grows into a generous, wide bush.

## Vegetative
Lemon balm flourishes on balconies when given a dedicated pot holding seven to fifteen liters with drainage holes. A standalone pot keeps the vigorous roots well contained. Place your container in full sun or gentle partial shade. Light partial shade keeps the leaves especially tender, juicy, and bright green without scorching. Water whenever the top centimeter of humus-rich soil feels dry, but avoid stagnant moisture. Feed sparingly with a thin layer of compost in spring. Excess nitrogen fertilizer creates watery foliage and weakens the fragrant essential oils. The sturdy square stems stand upright without any staking.

## Flowering
Tiny white-yellow to pale purple blossoms emerge from July through September. These flowers are an irresistible magnet for honeybees, bumblebees, and butterflies on sunny urban balconies. However, allowing flowers to form seeds causes rampant spreading across your garden containers. Snip off the flowering spikes as soon as buds appear, unless you plan to feed pollinators. Removing early blooms encourages your plant to keep producing tender, aromatic young leaves all summer long.

## Harvest
Gather your harvest on a dry morning once the dew evaporates. Pick stems right before flowering, when essential oils are at their absolute peak. Pinch tender top shoots frequently. You can even cut the entire plant down close to the soil multiple times per season, and it will quickly flush with fresh growth. Enjoy the leaves fresh, or freeze chopped leaves in water using ice cube trays to preserve their bright citrus punch. You can also infuse fresh sprigs into vinegar, syrups, or neutral oils. Drying on racks works too, though dried foliage loses some citrus vibrancy.

## FAQ
### How do I stop lemon balm from taking over my balcony containers?
Lemon balm spreads aggressively through underground root runners and massive self-seeding. In small spaces or balconies, plant it inside its own generous pot or use a root barrier. Always trim away flower heads before they set seed. This simple pruning keeps growth tidy and contained.

### What causes gray or brown spots on the lower foliage?
Mildew and leaf spots often appear during damp, still weather or when pots become overcrowded. Do not worry if bottom foliage turns gray or brown. Simply cut the entire plant right down to the ground. It will bounce back with clean, healthy green foliage within a few weeks.

### Can lemon balm survive Dutch winters outdoors in containers?
Yes, lemon balm is fully winter hardy in the Netherlands and Belgium. The green top growth naturally dies back in autumn, but the underground crown survives the winter without trouble. Fresh shoots will reliably emerge from the potting soil as soon as early spring temperatures warm up.

### Does lemon balm attract harmful insect pests to the balcony?
Most insect pests avoid lemon balm entirely. The intense citrus scent actively repels aphids, flies, and even mosquitoes. You only need to be mindful in early spring, when hungry garden slugs might target the tender, emerging shoots. Otherwise, it remains remarkably resilient and pest-free throughout the season.

### Where is the best spot for my container on a balcony?
Lemon balm adapts happily to either full sun or partial shade. On warm balconies, light partial shade is actually ideal. It protects the foliage from harsh heat, ensuring the leaves remain juicy, soft, and vibrant green without drying out or getting scorched by intense midday sunlight.

### How often should I water my potted lemon balm?
Water your pot whenever the top centimeter of soil feels dry to the touch. Lemon balm appreciates evenly moist soil, but sitting in stagnant water leads quickly to root rot. Always make sure your container has open drainage holes so excess rainwater or irrigation escapes easily.

### How should I feed lemon balm growing in a pot?
Lemon balm requires only modest feeding. Adding a thin one-centimeter layer of rich compost in spring provides all the nutrients needed for the season. Avoid heavy nitrogen fertilizers, as they force fast, watery leaf growth while diluting the essential oils responsible for that distinctive lemony fragrance.

### What is the best way to propagate an existing lemon balm plant?
Propagating is quick and effortless. In spring or autumn, split an established clump in half using a clean spade and replant the divisions. You can also snip semi-ripe shoots and place their stems in a small glass of water until fresh roots form before potting them up.

## Benefits
Lemon balm shines in the kitchen with its vibrant citrus aroma. Steep freshly bruised leaves in hot water for a fragrant herbal tea, pairing nicely with honey, mint, or elderflower. Drop leafy stems into carafes of cold water or homemade lemonades for crisp refreshment. In cooking, finely chopped leaves elevate strawberry jams, summer fruit salads, and baked goods. You can also fold the fresh herb into salad dressings, homemade herb butter, light pan sauces, and delicate fish dishes.
