## Starting
Komatsuna grows rapidly and copes remarkably well with cold weather. Sow outdoors from early April until May. For an early start, begin under cold glass in March. Avoid sowing inside a warm living room, as temperatures above 20°C and poor light create weak, leggy seedlings. Outdoors or an unheated greenhouse ensures sturdy starts. Seeds germinate within three to seven days between 10°C and 20°C. Long, warm spring days trigger premature blooming, so pause sowing during May and June. Start your second crop from mid-July until early September. In a cold frame or tunnel, you can sow right into October.

## Seedling
Starting in plug trays with one or two seeds per cell protects young greens from hungry birds and slugs. When the first true leaf appears after the cotyledons, prick seedlings out into individual nine-centimetre pots filled with rich potting soil. Let strong roots develop before moving them outside. If you sow directly in rows, thin seedlings to five centimetres apart for baby leaves. You can thin them further for full heads. Eat the thinnings fresh in salads.

## Vegetative
Komatsuna thrives in small gardens and balcony planters. In square-metre garden boxes, four full heads fit neatly in one grid section. Plants tolerate early spring and autumn sunshine, but prefer partial shade when summer warmth arrives. Two to four hours of direct or indirect sunlight daily supports lush foliage. Keep your potting soil consistently moist at all times. Drought stress quickly triggers early flowering. Because it only produces leaves, this green requires less nutrition than heading cabbages. Mix in some compost and organic cow manure pellets before planting. Plants stay compact and upright without needing stakes.

## Flowering
Increasing day length, heat, drought, or sudden cold snaps can trigger bolting. Do not discard flowering plants. The tender, fleshy flower stems and unopened yellow buds taste delightful, reminiscent of broccolini. You can also eat the open yellow petals as a salad garnish. After pollination, the plant forms long pods filled with round seeds. Note that Komatsuna crosses readily with turnip tops and pak choi nearby, making true-to-type seed saving difficult in residential neighbourhoods.

## Harvest
Harvest young baby leaves within 20 to 30 days after sowing. For mature dark green foliage and crisp stems, wait 40 to 55 days. Use a cut-and-come-again approach by snapping off the outer leaves at the base. Always leave the central heart untouched so fresh leaves keep developing for months. Alternatively, cut the entire head at soil level or pull the whole plant up. Freshly cut leaves wilt rapidly within hours. Pick them right before cooking for optimal crispness. Stored in a damp tea towel or sealed container in your vegetable drawer, they keep for several days. You can also blanch and freeze them.

## FAQ
### Can I sow Komatsuna on a warm windowsill?
It is best not to sow indoors at normal room temperature. Warm conditions above 20°C combined with indoor lighting turn seedlings spindly, thin, and weak. Instead, start seeds under cold glass in an unheated space or sow them directly outdoors. This cooler environment results in much sturdier, healthier plants.

### How do I protect my seedlings from slugs?
Slugs readily target tender emerging sprouts and soft leaves. To protect your crop, raise your containers on plant tables or elevated balcony pots. You can also pre-sow seeds in plug trays until seedlings develop strong root balls and true leaves before planting them outside into open containers.

### Why do tiny holes appear in the leaves?
Tiny holes are typically caused by flea beetles during dry, sunny weather. These small jumping beetles love feeding on young brassica greens. Keep your potting soil consistently damp to deter them. Cover your balcony pots or garden beds with fine insect netting to shield the plants completely.

### How can I stop caterpillars on my balcony?
Caterpillars come from cabbage white butterflies laying eggs under the foliage starting in early summer. The most effective barrier is covering your containers early in the season with fine insect netting. This physical shield keeps butterflies away from the leaves and prevents caterpillar damage altogether.

### Can Komatsuna survive winter frost?
Yes, mature plants tolerate light to moderate frost down to minus five or even minus eight degrees. Cool frost actually improves the flavour. The cold converts starches into natural sugars, leaving the harvested foliage sweeter and softer in texture. You can safely harvest fresh greens throughout winter.

### Why did my spring plants start blooming so early?
Komatsuna bolts quickly when stimulated by warming temperatures and increasing day length during May and June. Sudden cold snaps or dry potting soil also cause stress that triggers premature blooming. Maintain constant moisture and pause new sowings during the longest summer days to prevent this problem.

### Can I save seeds from my own balcony plants?
Saving pure seeds is difficult in home environments. Komatsuna belongs to Brassica rapa and easily cross-pollinates with nearby turnips, pak choi, and turnip tops over hundreds of metres. Seeds collected from balcony plants rarely remain true to variety if other brassicas grow anywhere in your neighbourhood.

### How much direct sunlight does Komatsuna require?
This leafy vegetable does not need intense sun all day. Two to four hours of direct or indirect sunlight provides enough energy for healthy leaves. Full sun suits early spring and autumn crops, but partial shade protects the plants from heat stress during warmer summer months.

### Is the plant still usable after it bolts?
Yes, bolted plants are still completely edible. Do not throw them out. The young, fleshy flower stems and tight yellow buds remain tender and taste very similar to broccolini. Even the open yellow flowers make a crisp, tasty, and colourful decorative addition to your dishes.

## Benefits
Komatsuna delivers a fresh, mild flavour with a gentle mustard bite similar to turnip tops. Enjoy young leaves raw in salads, sandwiches, or folded into traditional Dutch stamppot. As foliage matures, stems turn juicy and crunchy while leaves deepen in cabbage flavour. In stir-fries, cook the chopped stems first before tossing in the delicate greens with garlic, ginger, sesame oil, and soy sauce. The leaves also excel simmered in miso soup, noodle ramen, or braised with tofu.
