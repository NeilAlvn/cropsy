## Starting
Start your swede after the mid-May IJsheiligen period ends. You can continue sowing through June and into early July. Early spring sowing risks premature bolting during summer heat. Seeds germinate best between 15 °C and 20 °C, usually sprouting within 5 to 10 days. Pre-sowing in small pots or plug trays works brilliantly on balconies. This gives fragile sprouts extra shelter against outdoor pests before moving to big tubs. If you prefer, sow directly into moist compost outdoors. Keep the soil gently damp while waiting for green shoots.

## Seedling
Thin direct-sown sprouts once their first true leaves appear. If raising starts in trays, prick them out into individual 8 to 9 cm pots. Move plants to permanent containers when they stand sturdy at 10 to 12 cm tall with three to four leaves. When transplanting, keep the taproot straight without bending it. An unbent root orientation prevents knobby, deformed crops later. Protect tender young greens from visiting balcony slugs right away.

## Vegetative
Swede foliage expands vigorously, so choose pots holding at least 12 to 15 litres with a 30 cm diameter. In square-foot garden boxes, reserve a dedicated 30 by 30 cm square for each plant. Water consistently to maintain moderate moisture. Dry spells followed by heavy drenching will crack the roots or make them woody. Avoid high-nitrogen feeds, which encourage empty leafy growth and hollow centers. Instead, mix in well-aged compost and supply potassium in mid-summer for sweet, firm roots. The top root naturally rises above the soil line, so lightly earth up the base against gusty winds.

## Flowering
Swede is a biennial crop that stores sugars in its root during the first year. It naturally blooms during its second spring. However, early cold snaps or intense summer droughts can trigger premature bolting. If a flower stem shoots up, snap it off right away. Unfortunately, bolted roots turn stringy and tough to eat. If saving seed, overwinter a healthy root outside. It will produce bright yellow flower clusters the following May, followed by slender seed pods.

## Harvest
Harvest your swedes between October and January on a dry day. Light autumn night frosts convert starches into natural sugars, giving the yellow flesh a noticeably sweeter taste. Lift roots before severe freezing drops temperatures below minus 5 °C. Twist or trim the leafy tops away, leaving one centimetre of stem on the shoulder. Keep the skin and taproot intact to prevent rot. Do not wash the roots. Simply brush away loose soil. Store them layered in damp sand or wood shavings within a cold, frost-free shed between 0 °C and 4.5 °C for three to five months.

## FAQ
### How does swede differ from turnip and kohlrabi?
Kohlrabi forms a stem swelling completely above ground with pale or purple skin and crisp white flesh. Turnips, or meiraap, produce smaller, watery white roots that spoil quickly and dislike frost. Swede forms a heavy root half-submerged in soil, displaying dense yellow-orange flesh and remarkable winter cold tolerance.

### Why did my swede develop a hollow center?
A hollow center, or holle knol, happens when plants receive too much nitrogen. Heavy feeding causes rapid, uneven leafy growth spurts at the expense of balanced root development. Prevent this disorder by avoiding high-nitrogen fertilizers. Instead, enrich the soil with compost and mid-season potassium.

### How can I stop cabbage root fly from attacking?
Cabbage root fly larvae burrow into vulnerable roots and kill young plants. Protect your container crop by covering it with fine insect mesh immediately after sowing or transplanting. Keep the netting firmly sealed so adult flies cannot enter to lay eggs at the base of the stems.

### Why are my swede roots cracking open?
Cracking occurs when irregular watering disrupts root growth. If soil dries out completely and then receives heavy rain or excessive watering, the root expands too fast and splits. Keep soil moisture even and moderate throughout late summer to ensure smooth, unblemished roots that store well through winter.

### What should I do about cabbage caterpillars?
Cabbage white caterpillars and cabbage moths can quickly skeletonize swede leaves. Inspect your plants regularly and use fine insect mesh over the containers from the start. Mesh creates a physical barrier that stops butterflies and moths from laying eggs on the foliage, keeping the leaves intact.

### Can I grow swede in a square-foot garden box?
Yes, swede adapts well to raised square-foot systems. Because the plant develops massive foliage and roots weighing over a kilogram, allocate one plant per 30 by 30 centimetre square. This spacing ensures ample sunlight and nutrients while preventing root crowding in small spaces.

### How do I protect swedes from severe winter freezes?
Light frost sweetens the roots, but prolonged deep freezes below minus 5 °C cause cellular damage and rot. If harsh cold threatens, lift the roots from their containers. Store them unwashed in boxes filled with moist sand inside a frost-free cellar or shed.

### Do I need to stake swede plants on a windy balcony?
Staking is unnecessary because swedes produce strong, low crowns. However, the top half of the swelling root naturally emerges above the compost line. Lightly earth up surrounding soil around the root shoulder to stabilize the plant against strong gusts during blustery autumn days.

## Benefits
Thickly peel the tough outer skin with a sharp knife to uncover the golden flesh. Swede offers an earthy, sweet, nutty flavor that shines in traditional Dutch stamppots mashed with potatoes, butter, fried bacon, and nutmeg. Roast thick wedges in olive oil and honey, or simmer cubes in hearty winter stews alongside pumpkin and parsnips. You can also shred raw roots into crisp mustard slaws. Even the tender young leaves can be cooked like kale or spring greens.
