## Starting
Start your celeriac indoors from mid-February until early April. This crop needs a very long growing season of around seven to eight months from seed to harvest. Because the seeds are tiny, germination takes patience and often lasts two to three weeks. Since they need light to germinate, press them lightly onto moist seed compost. You can also cover them with a paper-thin layer of fine sand or vermiculite. Maintain a steady temperature between 18 °C and 20 °C. Colder conditions slow germination down considerably and cause early stress to the young plants. Keep the seedbed gently moist as you wait.

## Seedling
Prick out your seedlings around April once they show two to three true leaves. Move them into individual nine-centimetre pots filled with nutritious potting soil. Take great care never to plant them deeper than they stood before. Keep the root neck and central growing heart above the soil line, or they will form tangled roots instead of a round bulb. Harden them off gradually from late April or early May. Give them a sheltered outdoor spot during the day, but bring them indoors overnight.

## Vegetative
Plant outside after the Ice Saints, between mid-May and early June. Cold weather below 10 °C shocks plants into early bolting. Give each celeriac a pot holding at least fifteen to twenty litres, with a depth of thirty centimetres and good drainage holes. Position containers in sun or light partial shade. Use rich, moisture-retaining soil packed with ripe compost. These heavy feeders crave nitrogen and potassium. Top up with extra compost or organic potassium fertiliser during July or August. Keep the soil constantly moist to avoid fibrous flesh and hollow spaces. Let the bulb swell above the earth without earthing up. Leave green leaves untouched.

## Flowering
Celeriac is naturally a biennial plant. It focuses entirely on building its storage bulb during the first growing season. Flowers and seeds only develop in its second year after winter. However, early cold snaps before mid-May or severe summer drought can trigger premature bolting. The plant falsely assumes winter has passed and pushes up flower stems. If this happens, your crop is ruined. A flowering tuber quickly becomes hollow, stringy, and completely woody. Protect your plants from chilly nights and water shortages to prevent blooms.

## Harvest
Harvest your tubers from late September until November once they reach ten to fifteen centimetres across. Plants tolerate a light night frost on their foliage. However, you must dig them up before sustained freezing temperatures arrive below 0 °C, as frozen bulbs will rot. Trim the leaves while keeping the central heart intact. Brush away excess soil and side roots. Place the clean tubers into a box of slightly moist sand inside a cool shed or cellar kept between 0 °C and 5 °C. They will store well for three to four months. Eat the largest ones first.

## FAQ
### Why is my celeriac forming lots of roots instead of a round bulb?
This problem usually happens when you bury the seedling too deeply during potting on or planting out. The plant’s root neck and central growing heart must always remain well above the soil line. In addition, cramped containers or potassium-deficient compost can prevent a proper round tuber from developing.

### What container size do I need on my balcony?
Choose a large pot or tub with a depth of at least thirty centimetres and a capacity of fifteen to twenty litres per tuber. Always make sure the container has adequate drainage holes at the base. Giving each plant this volume prevents stunted roots and helps retain essential moisture.

### When can I safely move my plants outside permanently?
Wait until after the Ice Saints have passed, typically between mid-May and early June. Young celeriac plants exposed to prolonged temperatures below 10 °C suffer cold shock. This thermal stress tricks them into blooming early later in summer, which completely ruins the harvestable bulb.

### How do I prevent my celeriac from going to seed?
Protect young seedlings from cold spring nights by waiting until after the Ice Saints before planting out. In summer, never let the container soil dry out completely. Drought and early chill fool the biennial plant into thinking winter has passed, causing it to bolt and turn woody.

### Why is the inside of my tuber dry, fibrous, or hollow?
Inconsistent watering is the primary cause of hollow and stringy flesh. Celeriac needs constantly moist soil throughout its entire growing season. When pots dry out, growth halts abruptly. Keep the soil evenly damp to ensure steady, healthy bulb expansion without stringy or hollow pockets inside.

### How should I fertilise this crop during the summer?
Celeriac is a heavy feeder that requires generous levels of nitrogen and potassium. Start by blending abundant ripe compost into the potting mix. Follow up in July or August with an extra layer of compost or a potassium-rich organic fertiliser to support strong tuber bulking.

### How can I protect my young plants from garden pests?
Slugs pose a major threat to vulnerable young celeriac seedlings right after they are moved outdoors. Protect your plants immediately by installing collars or protective barriers around the base. Regularly inspect your containers to ensure garden snails and slugs cannot reach the tender young foliage.

### What should I do if brown spots appear on the leaves?
Brown spots with tiny black dots indicate Septoria leaf spot, which spreads in warm, wet summer weather. Pluck off and discard infected leaves as soon as you notice them. Prevent future outbreaks by watering directly onto the soil rather than wetting the foliage.

### Can I harvest the foliage while the bulb is growing?
You can pick an occasional leaf for flavouring soups, but avoid harvesting too much green foliage. The plant relies heavily on its leaves to fuel bulb swelling. Only clear away withered outer leaves, leaving healthy green foliage intact to nourish the growing tuber.

## Benefits
Celeriac brings a mild, spicy, and earthy flavour to the kitchen. Enjoy it raw, roasted, boiled, or puréed into soups. It is a star ingredient in classic pea soup, warming winter stews, and roasted vegetable traybakes. Grate it finely for fresh salads with apples and walnuts, or fry thick seasoned slices as hearty steaks. You can also toss the aromatic leaves into broths or dry them for homemade celery salt. Peel the tough skin thickly, and rub surfaces with lemon juice to stop rapid browning.
