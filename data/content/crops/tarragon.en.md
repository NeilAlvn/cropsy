## Starting
Start French tarragon from a bought organic potted cutting, often sold as ‘Senior’. French tarragon is sterile in our climate and produces no viable seeds. If you prefer Russian tarragon, you can sow seeds indoors from March to June at 18 to 20 degrees Celsius. You can also direct sow Russian tarragon outdoors from mid-May once the Ice Saints have passed. For true French tarragon, expand your stock by dividing mature clumps in spring or autumn. You can also take tender softwood cuttings during the summer months. Always choose French tarragon if you want the classic sweet anise flavor.

## Seedling
Prick out your Russian tarragon seedlings into individual pots once their first true leaves appear. Keep them indoors in warm conditions until mid-May. Both tender seedlings and young French tarragon cuttings must wait until after the Ice Saints before moving permanently outdoors. When planting in a square foot garden box, place your tarragon into its own corner section or a separate mini-pot. This perennial plant grows vigorously and needs dedicated room away from smaller herbs.

## Vegetative
Place your container in full sun and shelter it from harsh winds. Strong sunlight develops the plant's aromatic oils. Use a generous pot holding at least seven to ten liters or measuring twenty-five to thirty centimeters across. Drill drainage holes and add a base layer of potsherds or expanded clay pellets. Fill with light, well-draining soil mixed with ripe compost. Water moderately, letting the topsoil dry out slightly between waterings, as wet roots cause rot. Topdress with a thin layer of compost each spring. Avoid heavy nitrogen fertilizers, which soften stems and dilute flavor. Use a bamboo stake against balcony winds.

## Flowering
Flowering occurs during July and August with small, inconspicuous yellow-green blooms grouped in panicles. French tarragon rarely finishes blooming in our climate and never produces viable seeds. For maximum leafy production, clip away emerging flower stems promptly. Removing these buds forces the plant to channel its energy back into tender young shoots rather than wasted flowers. Regular trimming keeps your container plant bushy and neat throughout late summer.

## Harvest
Harvest fresh leafy stems from June until November. Pinch stem tips and pluck leaves regularly to stimulate fresh, bushy growth and prevent tough, woody stems. Tarragon retains its delicate flavor best when frozen. Strip fresh leaves into freezer bags, pressing out air before sealing. Alternatively, chop leaves into ice cube trays topped with water, broth, or olive oil. You can also infuse fresh sprigs into white wine or apple cider vinegar for one to four weeks. If dehydrating, keep the temperature strictly at or below thirty-five degrees Celsius. Excessive drying heat quickly destroys the herb's aroma.

## FAQ
### What is the culinary difference between French and Russian tarragon?
French tarragon offers a delicate, refined flavor featuring sweet anise, fennel, and a gentle bitter note. Russian tarragon grows taller and hardier, but its taste is distinctly flatter and far less pronounced. For top culinary results on your balcony, always choose the true French variety.

### Can I grow French tarragon from seed?
No, you cannot grow authentic French tarragon from seed. In our Dutch climate, the plant is sterile and never sets viable seeds. Any packets labeled as tarragon seed will yield Russian tarragon. Always purchase an established organic French tarragon cutting instead.

### How do I protect potted tarragon over winter?
French tarragon is only half-hardy and container roots freeze quickly. Cut back the foliage in autumn. Mulch the surface with straw, dry leaves, or fleece. Shift the pot against a warm house wall or shelter it inside a frost-free garden shed during deep freezes.

### What causes potted tarragon to wilt and die?
Tarragon dislikes persistent cold, wet weather and stagnant moisture. Standing water quickly leads to root rot, causing stems to droop and die back. Always select a container with bottom drainage holes, add clay pebbles, and let the soil surface dry between waterings.

### How should I manage powdery mildew or rust?
If your tarragon leaves show signs of powdery mildew or rust fungal infections, prune the entire plant back hard to ground level. Dispose of diseased stems cleanly. The plant will soon sprout clean, healthy new stems from its established root system.

### What are the frothy white patches on the stems?
These white foam nests are made by spittlebug nymphs, also known as froghoppers. While they look messy, they do not harm the health or growth of a mature tarragon plant. You can simply rinse the frothy residue off using a spray of cold water.

### How do I propagate an established French tarragon plant?
Propagate your plant by dividing mature clumps in spring or autumn. Gently lift the root ball and split it into sections for repotting. Alternatively, snip tender softwood stem cuttings during the summer months and root them in moist, light compost.

### Why do stems sprawl and how do I support them?
Tarragon produces angled, branching stems that can flop over in windy balcony conditions or when overfed with nitrogen fertilizer. Insert a small bamboo stake or a plant support ring into the pot. Regular pinching of the tips also keeps the growth compact.

## Benefits
French tarragon is a cornerstone of French cooking. It provides the essential flavor in classic Béarnaise and tartar sauces. Alongside parsley, chives, and chervil, it completes the traditional fines herbes blend. Use fresh leaves in dressings, homemade herb butter, poultry, white fish, shellfish, and egg dishes. It pairs wonderfully with mushrooms and green beans. Because of its intense, dominant anise punch, use it sparingly and stir leaves in right at the end of cooking.
