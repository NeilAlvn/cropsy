## Starting
Thyme seeds are tiny and sprout slowly. Sow indoors from March to April at warm room temperatures around 18 to 20 °C. Because thyme needs light to sprout, cover seeds with only a whisper of fine sand. Mist gently with a spray bottle. Starting seeds requires patience during their sluggish first year. You can also skip sowing entirely. Buy an organic potted starter in spring, or take semi-ripe cuttings in late summer. Wait until after the Ice Saints in mid-May before moving young plants onto your sunny Dutch balcony. This ensures the frosty spring nights are truly gone.

## Seedling
Thyme sprouts into delicate, wispy threads. When the first true leaves show, prick them out in little clusters of three to five seedlings. Pot them into lean cuttings compost. In early May, gradually harden off your young pots outdoors. Choose a sheltered spot with partial shade and protection from harsh balcony winds. Once the mid-May frosts pass, move the clusters into their final outdoor pots. Growing them in tufts gives your plant a full, bushy start.

## Vegetative
Give thyme its own dedicated container with generous drainage holes. Line the base with a layer of hydroleca or gravel to stop standing water. This Mediterranean shrub loves lean, gritty ground. Mix your potting soil with coarse sand, lava grit, or fine gravel. Never add rich compost or high-nitrogen feed, which causes floppy growth without scent. Water sparingly. Always let the soil dry out thoroughly between waterings. Thyme only needs extra sips during fierce heatwaves over 25 °C. Stems turn naturally woody and sturdy, so your balcony plant stays neat without stakes or supports.

## Flowering
From late spring into summer, thyme covers itself in tiny white-pink to lilac blooms. These nectar-rich flowers make your balcony a haven for bees, bumblebees, and hoverflies. Do not let the plant exhaust itself setting seeds. Once the blossoms fade, trim the plant back by about a third, cutting just above healthy green growth. Never cut back into old, bare wood, as it will not regrow. This post-flowering trim keeps the shrub compact and vigorous.

## Harvest
Snip fresh shoot tips throughout spring and summer. In the first year, harvest sparingly to let the roots settle. In later years, you can cut up to a third of the bush. The finest time to pick is a dry, sunny morning after the morning dew has vanished, just before midday sun evaporates the aromatic oils. Thyme flavor peaks right as flower buds open. To preserve your harvest, hang small bundles upside down in a dark, warm, ventilated room. Strip dry leaves into airtight jars, freeze sprigs in olive oil, or keep them briefly in the fridge wrapped in damp paper.

## FAQ
### Can I grow thyme in a shared vegetable planter?
It is best to give thyme its own pot. Vegetable planters usually contain rich, moist soil with compost, which harms Mediterranean herbs. Thyme needs lean, dry soil and minimal nutrients. Growing it separately also prevents vigorous leafy greens from overgrowing and shading out your compact shrub.

### Why does my thyme plant get woody and bare inside?
Thyme naturally turns woody if left unpruned. Without regular maintenance, the center loses light and leaves drop. Keep it green by trimming the tips each spring and cutting back by a third directly after flowering. Always prune above green growth; cutting into bare wood will kill that branch.

### How do I help container thyme survive Dutch winters?
Cold winter wetness is far more dangerous than frost. While thyme tolerates light freezing temperatures, wet roots will quickly rot. Lift your pots onto pot feet so water drains freely. Move the container close to a house wall or under a balcony shelter to shield it from persistent winter rain.

### Why do supermarket herb pots die so quickly at home?
Supermarket herbs are forced rapidly in tiny pots using high doses of fertilizer and water. They have fragile roots and weak stems. When you bring one home, divide the root ball into two or three pieces. Repot them immediately into gritty, well-draining soil without compost to help them adapt.

### How much fertilizer does container thyme need?
Thyme thrives in poor, lean soil and requires very little food. Avoid compost and nitrogen-heavy fertilizers, which cause leggy, scentless growth and invite rot. At most, provide a tiny sprinkle of organic fertilizer or lime in early spring. Neglect generally produces much stronger aroma than overfeeding.

### What should I do if powdery mildew appears?
Powdery mildew develops in stagnant, humid air or overly damp conditions. Place your container in a sunny spot with plenty of breezy airflow on your balcony. Always water directly onto the soil, never overhead onto the foliage. Allow the potting soil to dry out between waterings.

### Does thyme attract garden pests on a balcony?
Pests rarely bother thyme because its aromatic essential oils act as a natural deterrent. It can even help deter aphids from nearby plants. Wet conditions and poor air movement are your main concerns, as dampness can trigger fungal rot. Keep the plant dry and in full sun.

### How do I take cuttings from my plant?
Multiply existing plants in late summer using semi-ripe cuttings or layering. Snip healthy, non-flowering shoots that have begun to firm up at the base. Root them in a gritty cutting mixture. This propagation method gives you robust new plants much faster than starting from tiny seeds in spring.

## Benefits
Thyme is an indispensable kitchen classic with a robust, earthy aroma. Unlike delicate leafy herbs, its flavor does not fade during long simmering. Add whole sprigs right at the start of cooking for rich stews, tomato sauces, soups, and slow-baked oven dishes. It pairs naturally with garlic, rosemary, sage, oregano, and bay leaves on roasted potatoes or vegetables. You can also steep fresh sprigs in hot water for a fragrant herbal tea, or infuse sprigs into olive oil and vinegar.
