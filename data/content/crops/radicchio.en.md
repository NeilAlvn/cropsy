## Starting
Radicchio is a wonderful cool-weather crop for a Dutch balcony or patio. Start seeds between mid-June and late July, right around the longest day of the year. Sowing can continue until late July. Avoid starting earlier in spring or right after IJsheiligen, as lengthening days and early heat cause premature bolting. Seeds germinate reliably when soil temperatures sit between 15 °C and 22 °C. Growing late allows your plants to mature smoothly during the cooler months. This makes it an ideal succession crop for small outdoor spaces. Fill your pots and enjoy a productive late-season start.

## Seedling
Young radicchio forms a sensitive taproot and dislikes bare-root transplanting. Sowing directly in your container drills is a gentle choice. You can then thin out crowded seedlings three to four weeks later. If you prefer starting in module trays or soil blocks to save balcony room, keep the root plugs completely undisturbed when planting out. Protecting the delicate root ball ensures steady early development. Give each seedling plenty of open room to spread its broad leaves without disturbance.

## Vegetative
Choose a container with generous drainage holes, a depth of 20 to 25 cm, and a volume of 7 to 10 litres per plant. Place pots in full sun to light partial shade. Cool autumn nights combined with sunny days trigger rich wine-red leaf colours with crisp white veins. Always water at the base of the plant. Irregular moisture stresses radicchio and increases bitterness, while wet foliage invites fungal crown rot. Feed lightly with a modest dressing of compost. Avoid heavy nitrogen fertiliser to keep heads tight and firm rather than loose and leafy. Plants stay neat and need no staking.

## Flowering
Radicchio is cultivated solely for its crisp, tightly folded head of foliage. Plants do not need to flower. When triggered by high temperatures or stress, they bolt and send up tall stems with sky-blue chicory blossoms. If flowering starts, the head stops forming entirely, and the foliage becomes leathery and aggressively bitter. You should quickly pull bolted plants from your containers. Alternatively, you can leave the open blooms on your balcony as a lovely nectar source for visiting bees.

## Harvest
Harvest begins in autumn, running from September through November or into early December. Varieties like Rossa di Chioggia mature before winter, while Rode van Verona can overwinter under shelter for early spring harvesting. Gather your radicchio when the centre feels solid, dense, and compact. Cut the entire head right at ground level. Outdoor weather may leave the outer foliage green or ragged. Simply peel these outer layers away to reveal the ruby-red heart beneath. Light frosts down to -3 °C or -5 °C actually soften bitterness and increase sweetness. Keep unwashed heads lightly covered in your refrigerator drawer for two to three weeks.

## FAQ
### Why are my radicchio leaves still completely green?
Young radicchio leaves naturally emerge green during the summer months. Do not worry if your plants lack red coloration early on. The characteristic wine-red hue and crisp white veins develop only when late summer and autumn bring chilly nights combined with sunny days. This cool weather triggers red anthocyanin pigments.

### How do I prevent rot and mould during wet Dutch autumns?
Wet weather often encourages fungal issues like botrytis or smet. To protect container plants, elevate your patio pots onto pot feet so surplus rain drains freely. Clear away decaying or damaged outer foliage promptly to keep the plant base clean. Always apply water directly to the soil, avoiding the dense leaf rosette.

### How should I deal with slugs attacking container plants?
Slugs and snails find tender young radicchio leaves particularly tempting during wet autumn spells. Keep a close eye on your balcony pots and inspect the foliage regularly. Checking containers after damp evenings helps you spot pests early. Removing debris around containers also reduces hiding spots and keeps your autumn harvest protected and intact.

### Can radicchio stay outside during freezing weather?
Radicchio handles light frost very well, withstanding cold drops down to -3 °C or -5 °C. A brief cold snap actually improves the flavour by mellowing bitterness and highlighting sweetness. If harsh freezes threaten, move pots under shelter. Certain varieties like Rode van Verona easily overwinter sheltered for early spring harvests.

### Why did my radicchio plant bolt and produce blue flowers?
Bolting is caused by stress, high summer heat, or sowing too early in spring under lengthening days. When this happens, the plant sends up tall stems bearing sky-blue chicory flowers. The central head stops forming and the foliage turns leathery and bitter. Pull bolted plants or leave them for foraging bees.

### How should I fertilise radicchio in a balcony pot?
Radicchio is a low-to-moderate feeder that does not require heavy nutrients. A modest dressing of compost mixed into the container soil provides plenty of nourishment. Avoid fertilisers high in nitrogen. Too much nitrogen encourages loose, leafy open rosettes that easily succumb to rot, preventing tight, crisp hearts from forming properly.

### What size pot should I choose for each plant?
Choose a container offering at least seven to ten litres of volume for each plant, paired with a minimum depth of 20 to 25 cm. This depth allows radicchio to develop its sensitive taproot properly. Always verify that the pot features generous drainage holes to prevent soggy soil around roots.

### How long can I store harvested heads in the fridge?
Freshly harvested radicchio keeps exceptionally well after cutting. Place the unwashed heads in the vegetable drawer of your refrigerator. Cover them lightly to stop the crisp leaves from dehydrating in the cold air. Stored this way, heads remain firm, fresh, and delicious to use for up to two or three weeks.

## Benefits
Radicchio brings wonderful culinary versatility to autumn meals. Enjoy it raw in crisp salads, where its pleasant bitterness balances sweet apple, pear, citrus, and aged balsamic vinegar. It complements walnuts, pecans, and soft cheeses like goat cheese, feta, or gorgonzola. For warm dishes, sear halved heads in olive oil, roast them in the oven, or grill them. Shredding leaves into a creamy risotto mellows their sharp bite, coaxing out a rich, nutty sweetness celebrated in traditional Italian cooking.
