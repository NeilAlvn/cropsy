## Starting
Direct sowing outdoors is best for chamomile. Sowing straight into pots protects the sensitive taproot from transplant shock. Start in early spring from March to April, or sow in autumn. Chamomile is a light germinator. Simply sprinkle the tiny seeds over fine compost and gently press them down. Do not bury them. At temperatures of 15 °C or warmer, green sprouts appear within two to three weeks. Seedlings handle light spring frosts outdoors quite well. If you start earlier in a greenhouse or windowsill, wait until mid-May after IJsheiligen before moving tender containers outside.

## Seedling
Tiny seeds often sprout very densely. Thin your seedlings once they grow their first sets of true leaves. Give each plant about 15 to 20 cm of space to develop strong roots. Chamomile acts as a pioneer crop on disturbed soil. Still, aggressive weeds easily outcompete young starts. Weed your containers regularly during these early weeks. Once your young plants branch out, their bushy foliage naturally shades the ground and suppresses unwanted weeds.

## Vegetative
Use pots holding at least 3 to 5 litres with good drainage holes. You can also plant clusters in a 10 to 15 litre trough. Position containers in full sun to light partial shade. Full sun creates the richest aromatic oils. Use moderately fertile, free-draining soil with a neutral pH. Avoid heavy nitrogen fertilisers. Extra feeding creates floppy stems, fewer blooms, and attracts aphids. Keep the soil moderately moist, letting containers dry out slightly between waterings. Plants grow between 30 and 50 cm tall. In breezy balcony spots, steady tall stems with a low ring of twiggy sticks.

## Flowering
Chamomile flowers generously from late May or June right through September. The bright blooms act as magnets for bees, hoverflies, and parasitic wasps. They also serve as a larval host plant for the chamomile moth. Blossom heads open in steady succession all summer long. Toward the end of the season, leave several mature flower heads on the plant. They will drop ripe seeds, ensuring fresh chamomile readily returns to your balcony containers every year.

## Harvest
Harvest flower heads on a warm, dry, sunny afternoon around 15:00. Look for open blooms with white petals sitting flat at a ninety-degree angle. This timing captures the highest essential oil concentration. Gently pluck or snip off just the flower heads without long stems. Regular harvesting encourages plants to push out new buds. For drying, spread the blossoms in a single layer over cheesecloth, screens, or unbleached baking paper. Keep them in a warm, dark, airy spot away from harsh sunlight. Once crumbly and dry, seal them inside dark glass jars away from moisture.

## FAQ
### Should I start chamomile in seedling trays or direct sow?
Direct sowing into your containers is strongly recommended. Chamomile forms a delicate taproot that dislikes being disturbed by transplanting. Sowing directly on site lets the roots establish naturally without setback. If you choose to start indoors, make sure you avoid exposing the young plants to outdoor cold until mid-May.

### Why should chamomile seeds not be covered with soil?
Chamomile is a light germinator. The seeds require direct light exposure to sprout properly. Simply scatter the fine seeds over your compost and press them down very gently so they make contact with the soil. If you bury them under dirt, germination rates drop significantly. Keep the surface consistently moist while waiting for sprouts.

### How can I tell true chamomile apart from false chamomile?
Look closely at the flowers and test their scent. True chamomile has a distinct, sweet, apple-like herbal fragrance, whereas false chamomile lacks this soothing scent entirely. If you slice open the yellow conical centre of the flower head, true chamomile has a completely hollow interior receptacle, while false chamomile has a solid, pith-filled core.

### How do I manage black aphids on my chamomile?
Black aphids often target young flower stems in early summer. You can gently wash them away with a firm jet of clean water. Encourage natural garden predators like hoverflies, ladybirds, and lacewing larvae to feast on them. Avoid applying heavy nitrogen fertilizers, as lush growth makes plants far more vulnerable to aphid infestations.

### What size pot works best for balcony chamomile?
Select a container holding at least 3 to 5 litres per plant, measuring 15 to 20 cm in diameter and depth. You can also group multiple plants in a larger 10 to 15 litre balcony trough. Always check that the pot has proper drainage holes at the base to keep excess water flowing freely.

### How do I prevent powdery mildew in late summer?
Powdery mildew tends to strike overcrowded pots during humid late summer weather. To prevent it, give your plants plenty of breathing room and avoid overcrowding the container. Always water directly at the soil level rather than wetting the foliage. Good airflow around stems keeps leaves dry and naturally discourages fungal issues from taking hold.

### What is the ideal time of day to pick chamomile blossoms?
Pick flower heads on a warm, dry, sunny afternoon, ideally around 15:00. This is the moment when essential oil concentrations reach their peak. Check that the petals have opened horizontally at a right angle to the stem. Avoid picking in the damp morning or during rainy weather, as flowers must be completely dry.

### How should I dry my harvested chamomile flowers?
Spread the fresh flower heads in a single layer on cheesecloth, a mesh screen, or unbleached baking paper. Place them in a warm, dark, well-ventilated room, keeping them far away from direct sunlight. Once the blooms are crumbly and dry to the touch, store them in airtight, dark glass jars away from damp air.

### Can I use chamomile as a natural plant spray?
Yes, you can make an organic plant-strengthening spray using chamomile. Brew an infusion or create a cold-water extract from the harvested flower heads. Spraying this liquid over other garden plants helps protect them naturally against various common pests. It is a traditional ecological practice used by organic gardeners to support plant vigor.

## Benefits
Chamomile brings a delicate, sweet, floral flavour to your kitchen. Brew a comforting tisane by steeping one tablespoon of dried flower heads in boiled water for two to five minutes. You can also use five to six fresh blossoms per cup. The mild taste pairs beautifully with honey, garden mint, or fresh lemon verbena. Beyond warm tea, infuse your harvested flowers into homemade table syrups and cordials combined with lemon, sweet fennel, and honey.
