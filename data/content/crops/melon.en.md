## Starting
Melons need a long, warm growing season. Start them indoors from early to late April. Sowing earlier often causes weak, stretched seedlings due to low spring light levels on a balcony. Mix seed compost with perlite or coarse sand to guarantee good drainage. Lay seeds flat or with the pointed tip down to avoid rot. Keep the soil temperature consistently between 20 °C and 25 °C using a heated propagator, heat mat, or warm windowsill. Steady warmth helps your melon seeds sprout smoothly before moving outside.

## Seedling
Melon roots are fragile and dislike disturbance. Start seeds in individual 9 to 10 cm pots or biodegradable containers to avoid bare-root pricking out. When seedlings grow three to four true leaves, move them gently into an intermediate pot if needed. Never plant outside before IJsheiligen passes in mid-May. Night temperatures must stay reliably above 12 to 15 °C. Harden off your plants over seven to ten days in a sheltered outdoor spot during the day, bringing them inside at night.

## Vegetative
Give each melon plant a large container holding at least 25 to 35 litres with drainage holes. Plastic pots help retain soil moisture on windy balconies. Position the pot against a warm, sunny, south or southwest-facing wall for six to eight hours of sun. Melons drink heavily during warm weather. Water early in the morning using lukewarm rainwater, keeping leaves and the stem base completely dry. Train vines vertically up a sturdy trellis or tall stakes. To encourage fruiting side branches, pinch out the main vine once it has four to six true leaves. Select two or three strong lateral shoots to train upward.

## Flowering
Female flowers show a tiny melon behind their petals, while male blossoms sit on simple stems. If pollinators are scarce on your balcony, hand pollinate in the morning by brushing a stripped male flower against open female blossoms. Dutch summers and containers cannot support heavy yields. Thin your vines to just one or two fruits per plant, pinching off subsequent female flowers. As your vertical melons swell to one or two kilograms, cradle each fruit in a nylon stocking or net tied securely to the trellis.

## Harvest
Your melon is ready when it fills the air with a sweet, pronounced fragrance from the blossom end. Check the skin behind the netting; it changes from dull green to yellow or cream-beige. Look closely at the stem base for small circular cracks. A ripe melon cleanly slips off the vine with gentle thumb pressure. Reduce watering one to two weeks prior to harvest so the rind does not split and the natural sugars remain concentrated. Enjoy picked melons fresh at room temperature within three to five days, or keep them chilled in the refrigerator for up to a week.

## FAQ
### How do I avoid powdery mildew on my balcony?
Powdery mildew appears as white powdery dust on leaves in late summer. You can prevent it by training vines vertically up a trellis to ensure good airflow. Always water early at soil level and avoid wetting the foliage. If the disease appears, quickly prune away and discard heavily infected leaves to stop spores spreading to healthy balcony growth.

### Why are my melon fruits splitting open?
Fruits split when sudden large flushes of water arrive after a dry spell. The juicy inner flesh expands faster than the outer rind can stretch. Keep your container soil consistently and evenly moist rather than oscillating between parched and soaked. Adding a layer of organic mulch over the pot surface also helps retain steady, protective moisture levels.

### How can I prevent stem rot or collar rot?
Stem rot strikes when the plant crown sits in cold, saturated potting soil. Plant your melon seedling on a slight mound so excess water runs away from the stem base. When watering, direct lukewarm rainwater strictly around the outer perimeter of the pot instead of pouring it directly over the vulnerable central root collar.

### Why are my flowers dropping without setting fruit?
Flower drop often happens when night temperatures fall below 14 °C or pollinator visits are low on enclosed balconies. Excess nitrogen fertilizer can also prompt dense leaf growth while causing blossoms to drop. Support fruit set by hand pollinating blossoms on warm mornings and switching to a potassium-rich tomato feed once flowers begin blooming.

### How do I deal with spider mites and aphids?
Spider mites and aphids thrive against warm, dry balcony walls. Encourage natural insect predators by planting pollen-rich flowers near your melons. If you notice an outbreak forming on the foliage, treat the leaves with potassium horticultural soap to keep pest numbers down without harming your developing fruit.

### Why should I limit my plant to only one or two melons?
Container roots have limited space, and the cool Dutch summer provides a restricted ripening window. Leaving too many developing melons forces the vine to spread its energy thinly. This results in small, flavourless fruits that fail to ripen before autumn chills arrive. Pinch out late female blossoms to concentrate sugars into your chosen harvest.

### Why is vertical training beneficial in small spaces?
Growing vertically on sturdy trellises, mesh, or stakes up to two metres tall is ideal for compact spaces. It saves precious floor room on balconies while maximizing direct sun exposure against warm walls. Elevating the plant also keeps leaves dry and increases air circulation, effectively shielding foliage from moisture-loving fungal problems.

### What type of pot works best for growing melons?
Select a container holding at least 25 to 35 litres, measuring 30 to 40 centimetres in diameter, with generous drainage holes. A lightweight plastic pot is preferable to unglazed terracotta on a sunny balcony. Terracotta dries out too rapidly in summer heat, whereas plastic helps retain steady, necessary moisture around the roots.

## Benefits
Fresh homegrown melons are wonderfully aromatic and sweet. Slice your fruit raw and serve it chilled, or pair it traditionally with cured ham and herbs. Toss juicy melon cubes into refreshing salads alongside feta, cucumber, fresh mint, and lime dressing. Because melons have low pectin levels, surplus harvests blend smoothly with lemon juice, sugar, and water into refreshing summer sorbets or granitas. You can also puree the sweet flesh with fresh garden herbs and lime juice for an elegant, chilled summer soup.
