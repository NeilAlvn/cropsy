## Starting
Choose true lavender (*Lavandula angustifolia*), such as 'Munstead' or 'Hidcote', for kitchen use. Kuiflavendel and lavandins contain excess camphor, making them taste bitter or soapy. Start indoors in seed trays from February to April at 18 to 20 °C. The seeds require light to germinate, so cover them only with a very thin layer of sand or fine soil. Expect slow, irregular germination over two to four weeks. Because early growth is slow, you can also buy organic nursery plants or take semi-ripe cuttings in August. Set trays in a warm, bright spot and keep the soil gently moist.

## Seedling
Prick out seedlings once they grow two to four true leaves. Move them into individual small nursery pots filled with low-nutrient, airy potting soil mixed with coarse sand or perlite. Keep them bright and moderately warm indoors through April. From late April, harden the young plants off on mild days outside. Move them outdoors to your balcony or small garden containers permanently only after the Ice Saints pass in mid-May, once late night frosts are completely gone.

## Vegetative
Lavender forms deep roots. Choose a generous pot equipped with drainage holes, lined with hydroton or gravel. Place your container in the warmest, sunniest spot, such as against a south-facing stone wall. Keeping it in a separate pot prevents it from growing too vigorously or shading neighbors in rich garden soil. Water deeply, then let the soil dry out completely before watering again. Constant dampness triggers root rot. Use lean, lime-rich sandy soil. Skip nitrogen-rich fertilizers, which cause weak, floppy shoots and dilute essential oils. Instead, scratch a little seaweed lime into the potting mix each spring.

## Flowering
Flowers burst open from June through August, often lasting into September. The vibrant blooms produce abundant nectar and pollen, making them fantastic magnets for honeybees, wild bees, bumblebees, and butterflies on your balcony. Cut off spent flower spikes immediately. Preventing seed development saves valuable plant energy. This simple habit keeps your shrub neat and encourages a light second flush of flowers in late summer.

## Harvest
Gather flower stems early on a dry morning once dew evaporates, or late in the day. Avoid the midday heat, which evaporates delicate volatile oils. Harvest when the very first florets start opening for the strongest culinary fragrance. Fasten stems with an elastic band into small bundles. Hang them upside down in a warm, dark, dry, well-ventilated space. They dry fully in one to two weeks, feeling completely crisp between your fingers. Strip the dried florets from the stalks and store them in airtight glass jars or dark tins. The fragrant aroma will last for months.

## FAQ
### Why should I avoid French lavender for cooking?
Kuiflavendel (*Lavandula stoechas*) and hybrid lavandins carry high amounts of camphor. In cooking, this creates an unpleasant, bitter, or soapy flavor. True lavender (*Lavandula angustifolia*) cultivars like 'Munstead' and 'Hidcote' offer a sweeter, refined floral flavor that enhances dishes without tasting medicinal.

### How should I prune lavender in the spring?
Prune firmly between late February and early April after severe frost passes. Shape the shrub into compact mounds. Never cut back into old, bare wood. Always leave green shoots visible on every branch, because bare wood cannot sprout fresh growth and will simply die back.

### What is the purpose of summer pruning?
Summer pruning is a light trim carried out in August or September. Snip off all faded flower stalks along with a tiny bit of green shoot. This neat trim prevents winter snow or heavy rain damage, preserves shape, and readies the bush for the cold months ahead.

### Why is the base of my lavender turning woody and bare?
Lavender naturally becomes woody over time if left unpruned for several years. The overgrown center spreads wide, losing lower leaves and creating an exposed, brittle framework. This neglected structure easily splits open under heavy rain or winter snow. Regular annual pruning keeps foliage dense, low, and healthy.

### How often should I water container lavender?
Water deeply, then wait until the potting medium dries out completely before adding more moisture. Lavender features narrow, wax-coated grey-green leaves built to survive dry periods. Excessively wet potting soil rapidly rots the roots, so reliable drainage holes and loose, sandy soil are vital for container health.

### Can I plant lavender directly into my vegetable patch?
It is best kept in an isolated container on your patio or plot. Moist, rich vegetable soils cause lavender to grow too quickly, producing soft, leafy growth with weak aroma. In close quarters, mature lavender can also easily cast too much shade over neighboring crops.

### What causes lavender foliage to suddenly wilt and turn brown?
Sudden browning and wilting almost always points to root rot (*Phytophthora*). This problem occurs when the plant sits in waterlogged compost with poor drainage. Always ensure pots have clear drain holes, incorporate coarse gravel or perlite, and let the soil dry out thoroughly between waterings.

### How do I manage rosemary beetles on balcony plants?
Rosemary beetles (*Chrysolina americana*) are distinct striped pests that feed on lavender leaves. Control them easily without chemicals. Hold a small container beneath an infested branch and tap the stem firmly. The beetles quickly drop into the container, allowing you to remove them by hand.

## Benefits
Use culinary lavender sparingly, as its concentrated oils easily overpower recipes. Blend dried florets into sugar for baking, desserts, yogurt, or tea. Simmer flowers into syrups for homemade lemonades, add fresh blossoms to ice cubes, or infuse them in cream for ice cream. Brew dried flowers for ten minutes as a bedtime herbal tisane. For savory cooking, mix chopped leaves and flowers into marinades for lamb or poultry, or blend them into traditional Herbes de Provence.
