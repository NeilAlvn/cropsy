## Starting
Red cabbage starts easily on your Dutch balcony or patio. Sow early crops indoors or under glass in seed trays from February to March. For an autumn or storage harvest, sow either indoors or directly outside from April until early June. Cover the seeds lightly with fine soil. Red cabbage germinates even in chilly weather from five degrees Celsius, though it prefers temperatures between fifteen and eighteen degrees. Seeds usually sprout within ten to twenty days. Starting in small seed trays keeps your balcony organized.

## Seedling
Once seeds sprout, move them to a bright, cool spot immediately. Warm rooms above eighteen to twenty degrees cause weak, leggy stems that collapse. Thin seedlings out or prick them into individual pots once their first true cabbage leaves emerge. Harden them off outside for over a week during daytime hours to adapt to wind. Young plants tolerate light night frosts. Plant them out permanently between mid-April and early June, with Ice Saints marking the safest period.

## Vegetative
Give each red cabbage a pot holding at least twenty to thirty litres with drainage holes. If using garden square grids, allocate one plant per thirty-centimetre square near the edge. Cabbages are heavy feeders and love lime. Provide rich, moisture-retentive soil with a pH around 6.5 to 7.2, enriched with ripe compost and organic cow manure pellets. Water evenly at the base, because swinging between drought and downpours cracks the head. Mound soil around the base to prevent strong balcony winds from tilting your cabbage.

## Flowering
Red cabbage is a biennial plant that forms a dense head in its first season. Premature flowering happens when early spring cold is followed by heat and drought stress. Bolted plants will not form solid heads. If a healthy cabbage overwinters, the head bursts open the following spring into branched clusters of yellow flowers. These cross-pollinate easily with broccoli, kale, and white cabbage, making balcony seed production tricky.

## Harvest
Harvest your red cabbage from July until November, depending on your sowing time. Cut just below the head with a sharp knife when it feels compact, heavy, and firm. Always harvest storage crops before heavy frosts arrive. Frozen cabbages turn brown inside, stink, and rot during storage. You can store clean, undamaged heads with outer leaves for up to two months in a cool, dark, ventilated space. Alternatively, use traditional trenching with the roots intact. Sliced cabbage lasts only a few days in the fridge, but it freezes well after blanching.

## FAQ
### How do I protect against cabbage white butterflies?
Cabbage white butterflies are a common pest. They lay eggs beneath the foliage, and their caterpillars quickly strip the leaves or bore into the forming head. You can easily protect your balcony container by covering the cabbage immediately after planting with fine insect netting or lightweight curtain netting.

### What can I do against the cabbage root fly?
The cabbage fly lays eggs right at the base of the stem, and its larvae devour the roots. You can deter them by slipping a protective collar made of rubber or felt around the stem base. Alternatively, plant young seedlings slightly tilted or almost flat in the hole.

### How do I prevent clubroot in small gardens?
Clubroot is a persistent soil fungus causing thick swelling on the roots. It thrives in acidic ground. Prevent it by growing in soil with a lime-rich pH above 6.5 and maintaining a four-to-six-year crop rotation for brassicas. Using fresh potting compost in containers easily avoids this problem.

### Why is my cabbage head cracking open?
Cabbage heads crack when they face sudden shifts between dry soil and heavy rain. Consistent watering at the plant base prevents this issue. If heavy late-summer rain threatens a mature cabbage, grip the head with two hands and twist it a quarter turn to break surface roots.

### Can I plant red cabbage in a square-foot garden grid?
Yes, you can grow red cabbage in a square-foot garden layout by assigning one plant per thirty-centimetre square. Outer leaves will spread over neighboring spaces, so place your cabbage along the edge of the planter box or distribute them across different containers for better airflow.

### How do I stop birds and slugs from eating young plants?
Wood pigeons and hungry slugs eagerly devour tender young cabbage shoots on open balconies or garden borders. Covering your pots with fine insect mesh or protective netting keeps pigeons away effectively. Always check containers regularly to catch and remove any slugs hiding near the moist soil.

### Why do my indoor seedlings look tall and floppy?
Indoor seedlings stretch and become leggy when their room is too warm and lacks sufficient daylight. Temperatures above eighteen to twenty degrees Celsius cause weak stems that fall over. Move sprouted seedlings straight to a cool, exceptionally bright spot, and prick them out into individual pots quickly.

### How should I store whole red cabbages after harvest?
Keep undamaged whole cabbages with their outer wrapper leaves in a dark, well-ventilated, cool place between two and six degrees Celsius for up to two months. Ensure you harvest before severe frosts strike. You can also trench cabbages with roots in a cool cellar for winter.

### What happens if my cabbage bolts into flower?
Bolting occurs when a young plant experiences temperature stress, such as early spring cold followed by sharp heat and dry soil. Once a cabbage shoots into flower, it will no longer produce a tight, edible head. The plant instead focuses its energy on developing yellow floral clusters.

## Benefits
Red cabbage shines in home cooking. Stew it gently with apples, shallots, cloves, bay leaves, and cinnamon. Adding an acidic splash of vinegar or lemon juice preserves its vibrant red colour. For a crisp raw salad, slice it finely with carrots, mint, parsley, nuts, and figs in a bright citrus dressing. You can also pickle sliced cabbage in spiced sweet vinegar or ferment it into tangy red sauerkraut for tasty, long-lasting preserves.
