## Starting
Growing a fig tree in a container is ideal for Dutch balconies and small gardens. Restricting root growth prevents endless leafy branches and forces your tree to ripen fruit. Never sow seeds from shop-bought figs. They produce wild, barren trees or require non-existent pollinating wasps. Instead, choose a hardy, self-pollinating variety like 'Brown Turkey', 'Brunswick', 'Dauphine', or 'Rouge de Bordeaux'. Buy a rooted cutting or potted plant in spring. Move your pot outside permanently only after the mid-May Ice Saints pass. This protects fresh tender growth from damaging night frosts.

## Seedling
Propagate fig trees using hardwood cuttings taken in winter or leafy summer cuttings. Start rooted cuttings in a small pot using sandy, well-draining soil. Do not place a young plant straight into a giant container. Excess wet compost around delicate roots can quickly suffocate and rot them. Grow the sapling steadily for one or two seasons. Repot into a slightly larger container only when you see white roots emerging from the drainage holes.

## Vegetative
Mature potted figs need a sunny, warm spot sheltered from cold north and east winds. Position the pot against a south-facing brick wall for radiated evening heat. Choose a tub holding at least forty to fifty liters with drilled drainage holes. Add a layer of hydrogranules at the base. Use neutral or slightly chalky compost blended with coarse sand. In warm summers, large leaves transpire heavily, so keep the soil evenly moist without standing in water. Feed monthly from April until late July with potassium-rich organic tomato fertilizer. Stop feeding after July. You can easily train the supple branches into a flat fan against a trellis.

## Flowering
Fig flowers bloom entirely out of sight. The fruit itself is an inverted fleshy structure containing hundreds of microscopic blooms. Because Mediterranean fig wasps do not live in Northwest Europe, our varieties set fruit parthenocarpically without any insect pollination. Expect two potential fruiting cycles each season. The early breba crop overwinters as tiny pea-sized buds to ripen by July or August. The main crop forms on fresh spring growth, swelling for an autumn harvest if the late summer stays warm and bright.

## Harvest
Figs will not ripen once picked, so harvest them only when fully ripe. Check the fruit regularly in late summer and autumn. A ripe fig softens gently under finger pressure, sags downward on a bending stalk, and often shows a glossy drop of syrup at its base. Pick delicate fruit carefully with clean hands. Eat fresh figs immediately or store them in your refrigerator for up to three days. If your balcony tree yields an abundant crop, freeze sliced quarters for later use, dry them gently in a low oven, or simmer them into rich, homemade jams and compotes.

## FAQ
### Why is growing a fig in a pot better than in garden soil?
In open soil, a fig tree wastes energy producing vigorous branches and huge leaves. Restricting the root system in a container naturally curbs this excessive vegetative growth. Confined roots stress the plant slightly in a positive way, redirecting essential energy into setting and ripening sweet figs, even during cooler Dutch summers.

### Can I sow seeds from a supermarket fig?
Experts strongly advise against it. Seedlings take years to produce fruit and frequently turn out to be inedible male trees. Furthermore, wild figs depend on a specific Mediterranean fig wasp for pollination. That tiny wasp does not live in our climate, so your home-grown seedling will never bear edible fruit.

### How do I protect my potted fig tree from freezing winter temperatures?
Container roots freeze quickly once temperatures drop to minus five. During freezing spells, move your pot to an unheated shed, garage, or cold greenhouse. If the pot is too heavy to move, wrap the container in bubble wrap and protect the canopy using breathable fleece or reed mats. Wait until mid-May before bringing it out permanently.

### What should I do with small, unripe figs in autumn?
In late autumn, inspect your tree carefully. Any immature figs larger than a garden pea will not survive the damp winter and will merely rot. Snap these off by hand before winter arrives. Leave only the tiny, pinhead-sized embryo buds intact at shoot tips to develop into your early summer breba crop next year.

### Why does handling fig branches irritate my skin?
Fig branches and leaves bleed an opaque white sap when cut or torn. This latex sap contains ficin and furocoumarins, which trigger phototoxic skin reactions and painful blistering upon contact with direct sunlight. Always wear sturdy gardening gloves and protective long sleeves whenever pruning branches or picking ripe fruit on your balcony.

### When and how should I prune my potted fig?
Carry out structural winter pruning in February or March before spring sap starts flowing. Remove damaged, crossing, or excessively long branches to let light reach the center. In June, practice summer pruning by pinching back vigorous green shoots to four to six leaves, concentrating energy directly into the ripening summer fruit.

### Why are leaves turning yellow and dropping off?
Complete leaf shedding in late autumn is completely normal, as figs are deciduous trees preparing for winter dormancy. However, if your tree drops yellow leaves during the active summer growing season, check the roots immediately. Summer leaf drop typically signals sudden soil drought or waterlogged roots caused by blocked container drainage holes.

### Which fig varieties thrive best in the Dutch climate?
Choose cold-hardy, self-fertile varieties bred for northern maritime regions. 'Brown Turkey' is by far the most reliable and widely grown option. Other exceptional cultivars that ripen reliably in containers include 'Brunswick', 'Dauphine', 'Rouge de Bordeaux' (also known as 'Pastilière'), and 'Osborn Prolific'. These cultivars produce bountiful fruit without needing insect pollination.

## Benefits
Enjoy sun-warmed figs whole with their tender skins straight from your container. Fresh figs make stunning salads when tossed with peppery arugula, warm goat cheese, crunchy walnuts, and dark balsamic glaze. Cut a cross in ripe fruit, stuff with gorgonzola or roquefort, wrap with parma or serrano ham, and grill briefly. For desserts, bake them into rustic galettes or caramelize them with fragrant thyme and honey. Simmer surplus fruit into spiced jams and chutneys to complement hearty cheese platters.
