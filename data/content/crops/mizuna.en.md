## Starting
Mizuna is a reliable cool-season green for your balcony containers. You can sow outdoors from April straight through September. Unlike summer crops, you do not need to wait for IJsheiligen because it tolerates cold weather easily. Sowing in a cold frame or greenhouse is possible as early as February or into late autumn. Seeds emerge in 6 to 10 days when soil stays between 10 °C and 20 °C. Give your pots just 2 to 4 hours of sunlight daily. Shady spots work wonderfully and help protect plants from summer heat stress. Keep the potting soil evenly moist after sowing.

## Seedling
Seedlings develop delicate young taproots. Direct sowing in your balcony containers is ideal to avoid root shock. If using module trays, transplant very carefully before roots become bound. Thin your seedlings once they show two to four true leaves. For a square-foot container box, thin down to nine plants per square for medium rosettes. You can leave them dense if you plan to harvest exclusively as baby salad greens. Keep the soil gently watered during this early stage.

## Vegetative
Choose containers at least 15 to 20 cm deep with good drainage holes. Fill them with loose, humus-rich potting compost mixed with perlite or coco coir. True to its name, which means water greens, Mizuna requires consistently moist soil. Water regularly at the base of the plant to keep foliar mildew away. Never let the container dry out completely during warm spells, as drought causes bitter leaves. Mizuna is a light feeder that thrives on modest compost. Avoid high nitrogen fertilisers, which produce weak, pest-prone foliage. A light feed of organic liquid fertiliser midway through growth gives your plants plenty of strength.

## Flowering
Summer heat above 20 to 22 °C and long days encourage plants to bolt. Mizuna will shoot up tall stems carrying small, pale-yellow, four-petaled flowers. While flowering makes older foliage tougher and more peppery, both the flower buds and blossoms are fully edible. Scatter them across fresh dishes for a crisp bite. Once the plant flowers heavily, clear it out and re-sow for autumn. Note that it crosses easily with other flowering Brassica rapa varieties.

## Harvest
You can start snipping tender baby leaves after just three to four weeks. Full-sized rosettes are ready around five to six weeks after sowing. For cut-and-come-again harvesting, take sharp scissors and snip leaves two to three centimetres above soil level. Always leave the central growing heart untouched so the plant can regenerate. This method rewards you with three to five successive cuts. Alternatively, pick individual outer leaves continuously or slice off the entire head. Eat your harvest immediately for the best texture. Otherwise, store the greens in your refrigerator wrapped in a damp tea towel or inside an airtight container for three to four days.

## FAQ
### How much sunlight does Mizuna need on a balcony?
Mizuna thrives with very little direct sunlight. It requires just two to four hours of sun each day. It performs exceptionally well in partial shade. In fact, placing your container in light shade during warmer months protects the crop from heat stress and prevents premature bolting.

### Can Mizuna survive Dutch frosts?
Yes, Mizuna is an exceptionally hardy crop that can withstand temperatures dropping down to -5 °C or even -10 °C. Unlike tender summer crops, a light frost will not harm it. Cold weather actually softens the foliage and sweetens the overall flavour of the leaves.

### How should I protect my plants from flea beetles?
Flea beetles are common jumping pests that chew small holes through the leaves during warm, dry weather. To deter them, keep your container soil consistently moist, as these beetles dislike damp environments. You can also cover your crops with ultra-fine insect mesh to keep them completely off the foliage.

### What causes Mizuna to taste bitter?
Bitterness is typically triggered by dry soil conditions. Mizuna requires consistently moist potting mix to grow sweet, crisp leaves. Allowing your containers to dry out during warm weather quickly results in a bitter taste and can prompt the plant to bolt prematurely into flower.

### Can I plant Mizuna where cabbages previously grew?
No, you should practice proper crop rotation. As a member of the cabbage family, Mizuna should not be planted in soil where cabbages, radishes, or rocket grew in the previous three to four years. This rotational break effectively protects your container plants from soil-borne diseases like clubroot.

### How do I protect balcony seedlings from slugs?
Wet spring and autumn weather brings out hungry slugs, which target tender young seedlings. You can safeguard your container plants by applying copper tape around the rims of your pots. Elevating planters off the balcony floor is another practical way to keep wandering slugs away from your crop.

### Is Mizuna a heavy feeder?
No, Mizuna is a light feeder. It thrives on residual soil fertility from earlier crops or compost-enriched soil. Adding too much nitrogen can backfire by producing overly soft leaves that attract pests and diseases. A modest dose of organic liquid fertilizer midway through growth is entirely sufficient.

### Are Mizuna flowers edible once the plant bolts?
Yes, the small, pale-yellow blossoms and tender flower buds are completely edible. While bolting makes the older leaves tougher and more peppery, the flowers make a lovely, peppery addition sprinkled over salads. Harvest them fresh before clearing out the spent plant.

### How do I save true-to-type seeds from Mizuna?
Mizuna cross-pollinates readily with other plants within the Brassica rapa species, such as pak choi, turnip greens, and turnips. If you wish to save pure seeds for future growing seasons, ensure that there are no other flowering brassica varieties nearby during the blooming period.

## Benefits
Mizuna brings a fresh, crisp, mildly peppery mustard flavor to your kitchen, gentler than wild rocket. Enjoy younger leaves raw in mixed salads, tucked into sandwiches, or paired with soft goat cheese, chicory, and citrus dressings. Larger, mature leaves hold up well to cooking. Quickly stir-fry them in a wok, toss them into hot Asian noodle broths, steam them gently, or wilt them into a classic Dutch stamppot just as you would use traditional raapstelen.
