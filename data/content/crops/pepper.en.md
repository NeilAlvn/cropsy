## Starting
Start your sweet peppers indoors between late January and March. Our Dutch growing season is short, so an early start makes all the difference. Seeds crave steady warmth to sprout. Keep them between 20 °C and 24 °C on a warm south-facing windowsill or inside a heated propagator. Sow the seeds shallowly into damp seed-starting mix. Take care with the watering can. The soil should remain consistently moist to the touch, but never soaking wet. Sitting in cold, soggy soil will cause the delicate seeds to rot before they can even break through the surface.

## Seedling
When your seedlings grow their first true leaves, prick them out into individual pots of potting soil. Always lift plants gently by a leaf, never by the fragile stem. Bury them so the bottom leaves sit just above the soil line to stimulate extra roots. Move them to maximum daylight with temperatures around 18 °C to 20 °C to prevent leggy growth. On mild early May days, carry them outside to harden off, but bring them inside every evening.

## Vegetative
Wait until mid-May after IJsheiligen passes before shifting containers permanently outside. Ensure nights stay reliably above 10 °C. Choose plastic pots of 10 to 15 liters with drainage holes, as terracotta dries out too quickly in summer breezes. Fill with rich vegetable potting soil and add a two-centimeter compost mulch to conserve moisture. Stake the main stem to a bamboo cane against Dutch winds, as plants reach 60 to 90 cm. Water daily with lukewarm water during warm spells, pouring directly onto the soil. Feed with liquid tomato fertilizer every two weeks after a month.

## Flowering
To encourage compact, bushy growth on your balcony, pinch the top shoot above four to six leaves. Plucking out the very first crown bud redirects valuable energy into roots and leafy stems, ensuring heavier yields later. Sweet peppers are self-fertile, but a gentle tap on the stem helps release pollen in sheltered corners. Keep an eye on local night temperatures. Setting fruit demands daytime warmth above 18 °C to 20 °C, and cold nights below 12 °C can trigger blossom drop.

## Harvest
You can harvest your peppers green for a fresh, crisp crunch, or let them ripen into red, orange, or yellow for a sweeter flavour. Picking the earliest peppers while green often spurs your plant into producing a second crop before autumn chills arrive. Always snip fruit off cleanly with sharp shears, leaving a short two to three centimeter stem attached. Tugging by hand easily snaps brittle balcony stems. Store harvested peppers in a cool pantry around 12 °C to 15 °C rather than a cold fridge. Chop and freeze extra peppers raw, or roast them in oil.

## FAQ
### When can I safely move my pepper plant outside?
Wait until mid-May after IJsheiligen has completely passed. Sweet peppers cannot tolerate frost and need night temperatures to stay reliably above 10 °C. In early May, you can begin hardening them off by placing them outdoors during pleasant daylight hours, but always bring them back inside before chilly evenings set in.

### Why should I remove the first flower bud?
Nipping off the king flower—the very first central blossom—prevents the young plant from channeling all its early strength into a single fruit. Removing it redirects vital energy into broader root growth and sturdy foliage. This simple step ultimately rewards you with a bushier plant and a far heavier harvest later in the season.

### What causes black, sunken spots on the bottom of my peppers?
This condition is blossom end rot, a common issue in container gardening. It is caused by irregular watering, which disrupts the transport of calcium to the developing fruits. Prevent it by keeping your potting soil evenly moist. Check your containers daily during warm spells and never let the soil dry out completely between waterings.

### Why are my balcony pepper flowers dropping off?
Blossom drop typically occurs when temperatures swing too low. Sweet peppers require daytime temperatures above 18 °C to 20 °C and night temperatures above 12 °C to 15 °C to set fruit successfully. If chilly Dutch nights arrive, flowers may simply drop without setting fruit. In sheltered balcony corners, gently tapping stems can also assist pollination.

### How do I protect my peppers from sunscald?
Sunscald causes pale, papery patches on the fruit skin when peppers are hit directly by intense midday sun. To protect them during heatwaves above 30 °C, shift movable balcony containers into light partial shade. Additionally, avoid pruning away healthy leaves unnecessarily, as the upper foliage acts as a natural sun umbrella for developing fruit below.

### How do I tackle aphids and spider mites on my balcony?
Aphids and spider mites often target pepper plants grown in sheltered balcony nooks or indoors on sunny windowsills. You can manage these infestations naturally by spraying foliage with insecticidal soap or garlic extract solutions. Introducing predatory insects is another effective organic approach. Regularly inspect the undersides of leaves so you can tackle pests early.

### What should I do if my peppers stay green in autumn?
Cool September weather often slows ripening outdoors in the Netherlands. If damp, cold conditions stall colour development, move your containers indoors or into a sunny glass conservatory to finish ripening. Alternatively, pick the crisp green peppers anyway. Harvesting the first flush green is completely edible and helps spur the plant into continuing production.

### What is the best container size for growing peppers?
Choose a container with a minimum volume of 10 to 15 liters, measuring at least 25 to 30 centimeters in diameter and depth. Always ensure there are drainage holes at the base. Plastic pots are preferable to porous terracotta on Dutch balconies, as terracotta dries out too rapidly under dry summer winds.

## Benefits
Fresh homegrown peppers bring vibrant sweetness straight to your kitchen table. Crisp pointed varieties are ideal for snacking raw or packing into lunchboxes, thanks to their thin skins. Thicker blocky types bake wonderfully when stuffed with seasoned rice, minced meat, or lentils. Toss sliced peppers into wok dishes, slow-simmered ratatouilles, and summer pastas. Roasting peppers directly over a hot flame blisters their skin effortlessly for easy peeling, infusing the tender flesh with a rich, smoky taste for hearty spreads and salads.
