## Starting
Starting mint from seed is possible indoors in March at room temperature, around 18 to 20 °C. However, seeds are slow to start, and hybrid varieties like peppermint do not come true from seed. Vegetative propagation is much easier. Take stem tip cuttings or divide rhizomes in April or May when spring growth resumes. Roots form rapidly in plain water or moist soil. You can also split supermarket or garden nursery pots into smaller clumps and pot them into fresh compost. If you sow seeds directly, barely cover them with a very light dusting of soil or seed pads. Seeds need light and continuous moisture.

## Seedling
Prick out your young seedlings into individual pots once they reach six to seven centimeters in height and form true leaves. Grow them in cool, bright conditions rather than on a hot windowsill. This keeps the young stems compact and stocky instead of leggy. While mature plants tolerate winter cold, soft seedlings and fresh cuttings are sensitive to frost. Harden them off gradually. Move your containers permanently outside onto your balcony around mid-May, known as IJsheiligen, once late spring frosts have passed.

## Vegetative
Always grow mint in dedicated pots or fabric containers with drainage holes. Never plant it freely in garden soil or open beds. Aggressive rhizomes will quickly overrun your space. If planting in a raised bed, sink a deep pot or bottomless bucket into the dirt with its rim extending two to three centimeters above the surface. Mint loves cool, constantly moist soil. Water regularly so it never dries out, preventing wilting and premature bolting. Stems grow upright and sturdy without staking. Feed every two to four weeks with organic liquid fertilizer, and pinch tips regularly to encourage bushy, self-supporting growth.

## Flowering
Clusters of lilac, pale purple, or white flowers appear from July through September. These blossoms are a rich nectar source for bumblebees, wild bees, hoverflies, and butterflies on your balcony. Aromatic oils peak before blooming begins. Flowering turns leaves slightly bitter and stems woody. Cut the entire plant back hard once flower buds appear. This stimulates a fresh flush of tender, flavorful shoots for late-season harvesting. Avoid saving seeds, as mint cross-pollinates readily; stick to dividing roots instead.

## Harvest
Harvest fresh stems continuously from April until autumn. Cut stems just above a pair of healthy leaves rather than plucking individual foliage. This pruning encourages dormant side buds to branch out vigorously. Keep fresh stems in a glass of water on your kitchen counter, or wrap them in damp paper in the refrigerator. For drying, gather stems on a warm, dry morning once dew evaporates. Hang loose bundles upside down in a warm, dark, well-ventilated space. You can also freeze whole leaves in ice cube trays with water or chop them for frozen smoothie portions.

## FAQ
### Why should mint never be planted directly in garden soil?
Mint spreads aggressively through subterranean rhizomes and surface runners. When planted directly into open beds or borders, it quickly overpowers and crowds out neighboring plants. Confining mint to its own dedicated container, or sinking a deep pot with a raised rim into the ground, keeps these invasive roots completely contained.

### How do I manage watering for container mint on a balcony?
Mint prefers cool, consistently moist soil and cannot tolerate drying out like Mediterranean herbs do. If the potting mix parches, stems quickly wilt, foliage toughens, and the plant bolts to seed prematurely. Ensure your container has good drainage holes, and water thoroughly whenever the soil surface feels dry.

### How often should I fertilize my potted mint?
Mint is an active, vigorous grower that rapidly consumes the nutrients within container potting soil. Mix compost into the potting mix at planting time. During the active spring and summer growing season, apply a liquid organic herb or vegetable fertilizer every two to four weeks to maintain lush, healthy foliage.

### What causes holes in mint foliage during spring and summer?
Small holes are typically caused by the mint beetle, Chrysolina coerulans, or its larvae. These metallic blue-green beetles chew large holes into the leaves during warm weather. Inspect your plants regularly and hand-pick both the adult beetles and their larvae immediately to keep them from defoliating your herb crop.

### How do I treat rust or powdery mildew on my plant?
Mint rust causes orange or brown pustules under leaves, while powdery mildew leaves a white coating. Stagnant air and humidity encourage these fungi. Cut affected stems right down to the soil level, throw the diseased foliage in the trash, ensure good airflow around pots, and water exclusively at the base.

### Does mint survive cold Dutch winters in balcony pots?
Yes, mint is a fully winter-hardy perennial throughout the Netherlands and Belgium. Its aerial stems naturally die back after the first hard frosts of late autumn. The root system remains alive underground through freezing winter temperatures and will push up fresh, vibrant green shoots early the following spring.

### Can I successfully grow mint from supermarket pots?
Yes, supermarket pots provide an easy start. The plants are usually crowded tightly together in small containers. Simply divide the root clump into several smaller portions. Replant each clump into a separate pot filled with fresh compost. Water thoroughly, and the roots will quickly adapt and produce vigorous new stems.

### Why does my mint taste bitter in late summer?
Mint foliage develops its highest essential oil concentration and best flavor before flowering. As flower buds form from July onward, stems turn woody and leaves develop a bitter taste. Cut the entire plant back hard to prompt the growth of fresh, tender, sweet shoots for your late-season kitchen use.

## Benefits
Fresh mint brings incredible versatility to the kitchen. Steep whole sprigs in hot water with honey for classic tea, or add leaves to cold water with lemon and cucumber for refreshing infusions. Finely chop foliage into Mediterranean salads like tabouleh, stir into creamy yogurt tzatziki, or blend into mashed pea spreads. Mint pairs naturally with chocolate chip ice creams, fruit salads, and syrups. Even the small lilac summer blossoms are edible, making lovely colorful garnishes over cold soups and desserts.
